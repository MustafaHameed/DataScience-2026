\section{The Claim, and Its Fine Print}

Every programmer knows a hash table is $\bigO{1}$. That sentence has three
things wrong with it, and this chapter is about all three.

\begin{definitionbox}[Chaining, Load Factor]
A \term{hash table} with \term{chaining} stores $n$ keys in $m$ slots; each
slot holds a list of the keys hashing to it. The \term{load factor} is
$\alpha = n/m$, the average chain length.

\smallskip
\term{Simple uniform hashing} is the assumption that each key is equally likely
to hash to any of the $m$ slots, independently of the others.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[Expected search cost under chaining]\label{thm:chaining}
Under simple uniform hashing, an unsuccessful search examines
$\bigTh{1+\alpha}$ elements in expectation, and a successful search
$\bigTh{1+\alpha}$ --- more precisely $1 + \alpha/2 - \alpha/2n$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\textbf{Unsuccessful.} The key hashes to a slot whose expected chain length is
$\alpha = n/m$, and the whole chain is scanned. With the $\bigTh{1}$ to compute
the hash and index, the cost is $\bigTh{1+\alpha}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Successful.} Suppose the keys were inserted in order
$x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}$ and we search for $x_{i}$, chosen uniformly. Appending at
the head, the elements examined before reaching $x_{i}$ are exactly those
inserted \emph{after} it that landed in the same slot. For $j > i$, the
probability that $x_{j}$ shares $x_{i}$'s slot is $1/m$. So the expected number
examined is
\[ 1 + \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=i+1}^{n}\frac{1}{m}
   = 1 + \frac{1}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}(n-i)
   = 1 + \frac{n(n-1)}{2nm}
   = 1 + \frac{\alpha}{2} - \frac{\alpha}{2n}. \qedhere \]
\end{proof}

\begin{alertbox}[Three things wrong with ``a hash table is $\bigO{1}$'']
\begin{tightnum}
  \item \textbf{It is $\bigTh{1+\alpha}$, not $\bigTh{1}$.} Constant only if
        $\alpha$ is held constant --- which requires resizing, which is
        \chref{amortised}'s subject.
  \item \textbf{It is an \emph{expectation}, not a bound.} The worst case is
        $\bigTh{n}$: all keys in one slot. Section~13.4 produces exactly that
        on purpose.
  \item \textbf{The expectation is over an assumption about your data.} Simple
        uniform hashing is not a property of the hash function alone; it is a
        property of the hash function \emph{together with the keys}. Section
        13.3 shows a perfectly reasonable hash failing on perfectly reasonable
        keys.
\end{tightnum}
\end{alertbox}

\section{Measured: the Assumption, and the Prediction}

\begin{worked}[Does the Distribution Look Like the Analysis Assumes?]
With $n$ keys scattered uniformly into $m$ slots, a slot's load is
$\mathrm{Binomial}(n, 1/m)$, which for large $m$ is Poisson$(\alpha)$. At
$\alpha = 1$ the predicted number of slots with chain length $L$ is
$m\,e^{-1}/L!$. \texttt{code/ch13\_hashing.cpp} with
$n = m = 1{,}000{,}000$ and a multiplicative hash:

\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
chain length & slots observed & Poisson(1) predicts & ratio \\
\midrule
0 & 368{,}092 & 367{,}879 & 1.001 \\
1 & 367{,}372 & 367{,}879 & 0.999 \\
2 & 184{,}047 & 183{,}940 & 1.001 \\
3 &  61{,}795 &  61{,}313 & 1.008 \\
4 &  15{,}035 &  15{,}328 & 0.981 \\
5 &   3{,}054 &   3{,}066 & 0.996 \\
6 &     510 &     511 & 0.998 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
\textbf{The whole distribution matches, not just its mean.} Seven ratios, all
within 2\% of 1. This is the strongest form of evidence for a modelling
assumption: not that the average came out right, but that the shape did.

\smallskip
\textbf{The longest chain was 9}, against $\lg n / \lg\lg n = 4.6$ --- the
classical order of the maximum load. So even with a million keys and a good
hash, \emph{some} lookup costs nine probes while the average costs 1.5. An
expectation is not a guarantee, and tail latency lives in that gap.
\end{worked}

\bottomrule

\smallskip
\textbf{Six predictions, six matches, to three decimal places.} This is as
close as an analysis and a measurement get.

\smallskip
\textbf{And note what the time column does \emph{not} do.} Probes rise by
$4.4\times$ from $\alpha = 0.25$ to $\alpha = 8$; time rises by only
$2.0\times$. Longer chains are contiguous-ish in memory and cheap to walk,
while the initial slot access is a cache miss paid once however short the chain
is. The step count and the clock disagree again, in the opposite direction
from \chref{structures}.

\smallskip
\textbf{A caution about how this was measured.} The first version of this
experiment probed the \emph{first} 200{,}000 keys inserted. Those sit at the
front of their chains, so it reported 1.096 probes at every load factor --- a
perfectly flat column that looked like a result. The fix was to sample keys
uniformly at random. A benchmark that samples its inputs in insertion order is
measuring insertion order.
\end{worked}

\section{When the Assumption Quietly Fails}

Simple uniform hashing is an assumption about the \emph{relationship} between
the hash and the keys. Real keys are not random: they are IDs, timestamps,
pointers, offsets into arrays of structs --- and they very often share a
stride.

\begin{worked[The Stride]}
\end{worked}

\begin{worked}[A Reasonable Hash Meeting Reasonable Keys]
Keys $0, 64, 128, 192, \ldots$ --- which is what the addresses of an array of
64-byte records look like --- into a table of $m = 2^{20}$ slots, which is what
``round the capacity up to a power of two'' gives you. One million keys.

\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lrrr@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
hash & mean probes & longest chain & slots used \\
\midrule
$k \bmod m$          & \textbf{31.0} & \textbf{62} & 16{,}384 \\
mix, then $\bmod\ m$ &           1.5 &           8 & 644{,}230 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
\textbf{The division hash used 16{,}384 of its 1{,}048{,}576 slots} --- 1.6\%
--- and left the other 98.4\% empty. Mean probes 31 instead of 1.5: a
20$\times$ slowdown, and the longest chain is 62.

\smallskip
\textbf{The arithmetic is not subtle.} $\gcd(64, 2^{20}) = 64$, so
$k \bmod 2^{20}$ for $k$ a multiple of 64 can only ever produce multiples of
64. Taking the modulus by a power of two keeps only the low bits, and the low
six bits of these keys are always zero. The hash discarded the only part of the
key that varied.

\smallskip
\textbf{Neither choice was foolish.} ``Table size a power of two'' makes the
modulus a bitmask, which is fast and universal practice. ``Hash is the key
modulo the table size'' is the textbook division method. Each is defensible;
the combination, meeting keys with a stride of 64, is a 20$\times$ slowdown
nobody wrote down.

\smallskip
\textbf{The defence} is in the second row: destroy the key's structure before
taking the modulus. The mix used here is MurmurHash3's finaliser --- three
shift-xor steps and two multiplications --- and it moves high bits down into
low ones, so no bit of the key is ignored.
\end{worked}

\begin{conceptbox}[Rules that follow]
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Never use $k \bmod m$ with $m$ a power of two.} It is a
        bitmask: it reads the low bits and throws the rest away.
  \item \textbf{If you use the division method, make $m$ prime} and not close
        to a power of 2 --- then a stride $s$ collides only if $s$ is a
        multiple of $m$.
  \item \textbf{Better: mix, then mask.} A multiplicative finaliser then
        $\&(m-1)$ gives you the fast mask \emph{and} uses every bit.
  \item \textbf{Hash the whole key.} A hash that reads only a prefix of a
        string collides on every key sharing that prefix --- and URLs, file
        paths and identifiers share prefixes constantly.
\end{tight}
\end{conceptbox}

\section{An Adversary}

Section~13.3's collisions were an accident. They can also be arranged.

\begin{worked}[Hash Flooding]
If the hash function is fixed and public --- as it is in every language's
standard library --- then the keys landing in any given slot can simply be
computed. For $h(k) = k \bmod m$, the multiples of $m$ all hash to slot 0. No
cryptography, no timing attack: arithmetic.

\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
$n$ & random keys (ms) & chosen keys (ms) & ratio \\
\midrule
 2{,}000 & 0.01 &   0.62 &  64.0 \\
 8{,}000 & 0.06 &  10.19 & 162.3 \\
16{,}000 & 0.13 &  41.95 & 313.5 \\
32{,}000 & 0.45 & 147{.}27 & 324.2 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
\textbf{The ratio grows with $n$} --- 64 to 324 --- because the chosen-key case
is $\bigTh{n^{2}}$ for $n$ lookups while the random case is $\bigTh{n}$. The
table has become a linked list, and every operation on it is a linear scan.

\smallskip
\textbf{This is a real attack, not a thought experiment.} It is called
\term{hash flooding}: send a web server a few thousand form fields chosen to
collide, and its parameter dictionary --- every request --- turns quadratic.
Published against PHP, Python, Ruby, Java and Node in 2011--12, and the reason
those languages now randomise their hash seeds at process start.
\end{worked}

\begin{conceptbox}[Universal hashing, and why it is \chref{quicksort} again]
\textbf{The defence is to stop having a fixed hash function.} Choose $h$ at
random, at run time, from a carefully designed family $\mathcal{H}$.

\smallskip
$\mathcal{H}$ is \term{universal} if for any two distinct keys $x \ne y$,
\[ \Pr_{h \in \mathcal{H}}\bigl[\,h(x) = h(y)\,\bigr] \;\le\; \frac{1}{m}. \]
That is, a randomly chosen member collides on any particular pair no more often
than a uniformly random function would.

\smallskip
With a universal family, Theorem~\ref{thm:chaining}'s expected bounds hold
\emph{for every input}, with the expectation taken over the \emph{choice of
hash function} rather than over an assumption about the keys. An adversary who
knows your source code still cannot choose colliding keys, because he does not
know which $h$ you drew.

\smallskip
\textbf{This is precisely \chref{quicksort}'s move.} There, randomising the
pivot converted ``$\bigTh{n\lg n}$ if your data is unsorted'' into
``$\bigTh{n\lg n}$ expected, on every input''. Here, randomising the hash
converts ``$\bigTh{1}$ if your keys suit my hash'' into ``$\bigTh{1}$ expected,
on every key set''. Same technique, same payoff: \textbf{an assumption about
the world, which can be violated, becomes an assumption about a random number
generator, which cannot.}
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{conceptbox}[Hash table or balanced tree?]
\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{3.6cm}L{5.0cm}L{5.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
& \textbf{Hash table} & \textbf{Balanced BST} \\
\midrule
\endhead
search, expected & $\bigTh{1}$ & $\bigTh{\lg n}$ \\
search, worst case & $\bigTh{n}$ (or $\bigTh{\lg n}$ with tree bins) &
  $\bigTh{\lg n}$ \emph{guaranteed} \\
ordered traversal & impossible without sorting & $\bigTh{n}$, free \\
predecessor, range query & not supported & $\bigTh{\lg n}$ \\
needs & a good hash for \emph{your} keys & only a comparison \\
degrades on & adversarial or structured keys & nothing \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\smallskip
Hash tables win on raw lookup and lose everything about order. Where a
worst case must be bounded --- latency budgets, untrusted input --- the
tree's guarantee may be worth the logarithm. Java's \texttt{HashMap} hedges:
chains longer than 8 become red--black trees, so the worst case is
$\bigO{\lg n}$ rather than $\bigO{n}$.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Saying ``$\bigO{1}$'' without saying ``expected''.} The worst
        case is $\bigTh{n}$ and it is reachable on purpose.
  \item \textbf{$k \bmod m$ with $m$ a power of two.} Measured: 1.6\% of slots
        used and a 20$\times$ slowdown on keys with a stride of 64.
  \item \textbf{Testing only with random keys.} Random keys make every hash
        look good. Real keys have structure; test with yours.
  \item \textbf{Treating the assumption as a property of the hash alone.}
        Simple uniform hashing is a claim about the hash \emph{and} the key
        distribution together.
  \item \textbf{A fixed, public hash on untrusted input.} That is the hash
        flooding vulnerability, measured here at 324$\times$.
  \item \textbf{Ignoring the maximum chain.} Measured 9 with a million keys and
        a good hash, against a mean of 1.5. Tail latency is not the average.
  \item \textbf{Benchmarking lookups in insertion order.} The first draft of
        this chapter's experiment did, and reported a flat 1.096 probes at
        every load factor.
  \item \textbf{Forgetting that keeping $\alpha$ bounded requires resizing},
        whose cost is amortised --- \chref{amortised}.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lab}[What Voids the Guarantee]
Reproduces all four tables. Expect thirty minutes; part C builds tables holding
up to eight million keys.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++]
// ch13_hashing.cpp -- what O(1) actually promises, and what voids it.
// Build: cl /O2 /EHsc /std:c++17 ch13_hashing.cpp    (see Appendix A)

// --- 1. A chaining hash table, as plainly as possible --------------
// Buckets of vectors. No resizing, no tricks: the table size is fixed
// so the load factor is exactly what the experiment says it is.

// --- 2. Two hash functions, both entirely reasonable ---------------
// "Modulo the table size" is the textbook division method and what
// most people write first. It is fine when the table size is prime
// and the keys unstructured, and catastrophic when the keys share a
// factor with the table size.
static unsigned h_mod(unsigned k, unsigned m) { return k % m; }

// A multiplicative mix (MurmurHash3's finaliser). It destroys
// structure in the key before taking the modulus, which is the whole
// job of a hash function.
static unsigned h_mix(unsigned k, unsigned m) {
    k ^= k >> 16; k *= 0x85ebca6bu;
    k ^= k >> 13; k *= 0xc2b2ae35u;
    k ^= k >> 16;
    return k % m;
}

// --- 3. The distribution the analysis assumes ----------------------
// With n keys in m slots and perfect scattering, a slot's load is
// Binomial(n, 1/m), which for large m is Poisson(alpha). The analysis
// of "expected O(1)" rests on that shape, so check the shape.

// --- 4. The same keys, a hash that meets the data's structure ------
// Real keys are not random: IDs, timestamps, pointers, struct
// offsets -- and they very often share a stride. Here every key is a
// multiple of 64, which is what an array of 64-byte records gives
// you, and the table size is a power of two, which is what every
// "round up to the next power of 2" gives you.

// --- 5. Load factor is the one knob you actually turn --------------
// Sample the keys at RANDOM, not the first 200,000. The
// first-inserted keys sit at the front of their chains, so probing
// them reports 1.096 probes whatever the load factor is -- which is
// what the first draft of this program did, and the flat column was
// the clue.

// --- 6. An adversary, who does not need to be clever ---------------
// If the hash is fixed and public, the keys that land in one slot can
// be enumerated. Here they are simply the multiples of m. No
// cryptography, no timing attack: arithmetic.
for (size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
    ak[i] = (unsigned)((i + 1) * m);   // all hash to slot 0
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What to notice.} Part 3 compares a whole distribution against Poisson,
not just a mean. Any broken hash can still produce the right average chain
length --- the average is $n/m$ whatever the hash does, since every key lands
somewhere. Only the \emph{shape} distinguishes a good hash from a bad one, and
part 4's table makes that concrete: both rows have the same mean chain length
by definition, and one of them is 20$\times$ slower.

\smallskip
\textbf{Then try to break it.} Change the table size in part 4 from $2^{20}$ to
the prime 1{,}048{,}573 and re-run with the plain \texttt{k \% m} hash. Predict
first. The stride-64 keys should now scatter well, because 64 and 1{,}048{,}573
are coprime --- which tells you that the division method is not the problem,
the \emph{power of two} is.
\end{lab}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item A chaining hash table's search cost is $\bigTh{1+\alpha}$
        \emph{expected}, under the simple uniform hashing assumption. Each of
        those three qualifications can fail.
  \item Measured: with a good hash, the chain-length distribution matched
        Poisson(1) to within 2\% across seven lengths --- the shape, not just
        the mean. Longest chain 9 against a mean of 1.5.
  \item Measured: probes per successful search were 1.125, 1.252, 1.500,
        1.996, 2.998, 5.001 against a predicted $1+\alpha/2$ of 1.125, 1.250,
        1.500, 2.000, 3.000, 5.000.
  \item Measured: $k \bmod 2^{20}$ on keys with a stride of 64 used 1.6\% of the
        slots and cost 31 probes instead of 1.5 --- because a power-of-two
        modulus keeps only the low bits, and those keys' low bits are constant.
  \item Measured: deliberately chosen colliding keys made lookups 324$\times$
        slower at $n = 32{,}000$, and growing. This is hash flooding, a
        published attack against every major language.
  \item Universal hashing --- choosing $h$ at random at run time --- restores
        the expected bounds for \emph{every} input, exactly as randomising the
        pivot did in \chref{quicksort}.
  \item Use a tree where the worst case must be bounded or where order
        matters; a hash table buys lookup speed by giving up order entirely.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}
