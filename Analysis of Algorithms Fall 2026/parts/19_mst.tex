\section{The Cut Property}

\begin{definitionbox}[Spanning Tree, MST]
A \term{spanning tree} of a connected undirected graph is a subset of edges
that connects every vertex and contains no cycle. It has exactly $|V|-1$ edges.

\smallskip
A \term{minimum spanning tree} is one of least total weight.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{definitionbox}[Cut, Crossing, Light]
A \term{cut} $(S, V \setminus S)$ is a partition of the vertices. An edge
\term{crosses} the cut if its endpoints lie on opposite sides. A crossing edge
of least weight is a \term{light} edge for that cut.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[The cut property]\label{thm:cut}
Let $A$ be a subset of some minimum spanning tree, and let $(S, V\setminus S)$
be any cut that no edge of $A$ crosses. Then any light edge for that cut is
safe: $A \cup \{e\}$ is still contained in some minimum spanning tree.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
An exchange argument, in the pattern of \chref{greedy}.

\smallskip
Let $T$ be an MST containing $A$. If $e \in T$ there is nothing to prove, so
assume $e = (u,v) \notin T$.

\smallskip
Adding $e$ to $T$ creates exactly one cycle, since $T$ is a tree and $e$ joins
two vertices already connected by a unique path. That cycle contains the path
in $T$ from $u$ to $v$, and since $u$ and $v$ lie on opposite sides of the cut,
that path must cross the cut at least once --- say at edge $e'$.

\smallskip
Now $e'$ is a crossing edge and $e$ is a \emph{light} crossing edge, so
$w(e) \le w(e')$. Form
\[ T' = \bigl(T \setminus \{e'\}\bigr) \cup \{e\}. \]
Removing $e'$ from the cycle leaves a tree spanning the same vertices, so $T'$
is a spanning tree, and
\[ w(T') = w(T) - w(e') + w(e) \le w(T). \]
Since $T$ was minimum, $w(T') = w(T)$ and $T'$ is also an MST.

\smallskip
Finally $A \subseteq T'$: no edge of $A$ crosses the cut, so $e' \notin A$, and
removing $e'$ did not disturb $A$. Hence $A \cup \{e\} \subseteq T'$, an MST.
\end{proof}

\begin{conceptbox}[One theorem, two algorithms]
Theorem~\ref{thm:cut} says a light crossing edge may always be taken. It does
not say \emph{which} cut to use, and the two classical algorithms are two
answers.

\smallskip
\textbf{Prim} takes $S$ = the vertices of the tree built so far. The light edge
is the cheapest edge leaving the tree, so the structure needed is a
\textbf{priority queue}.

\smallskip
\textbf{Kruskal} considers edges in increasing weight order; when an edge joins
two different components, the cut separating those components is crossed by no
chosen edge, and this edge is lightest among those remaining. The structure
needed is one that answers \textbf{``are these two vertices already
connected?''} --- disjoint-set union.

\smallskip
Both are greedy, both are optimal, and both are optimal \emph{for the same
reason}. The difference is entirely which data structure the chosen cut
requires.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Kruskal, and the Structure It Needs}

\dsfig{\aoaalg{\algname{Kruskal}$(G, w)$}{
\State $A \gets \emptyset$
\For{each vertex $v$}
  \State \algname{Make-Set}$(v)$
\EndFor
\State sort the edges by non-decreasing weight
\For{each edge $(u,v)$ in that order}
  \If{\algname{Find}$(u) \ne$ \algname{Find}$(v)$}
    \State $A \gets A \cup \{(u,v)\}$
      \Comment{safe, by the cut property}
    \State \algname{Union}$(u, v)$
  \EndIf
\EndFor
\State \Return $A$
}}{\algname{Kruskal}. The sort is $\bigTh{|E| \lg |E|}$ and dominates; the
$\bigO{|E|}$ union--find operations cost $\bigO{|E|\,\alpha(|V|)}$, which is
why the amortised bound matters.}{kruskal}

\begin{definitionbox}[Disjoint-Set Union]
Maintains a partition of the vertices under three operations:
\algname{Make-Set}$(v)$, \algname{Find}$(v)$ returning the representative of
$v$'s set, and \algname{Union}$(u,v)$ merging two sets.

\smallskip
Each set is a tree whose root is the representative. \algname{Find} walks to
the root, so its cost is the depth.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{conceptbox}[Two optimisations, both two lines]
\textbf{Union by rank.} When merging, attach the shorter tree under the taller.
This alone bounds the depth by $\bigO{\lg |V|}$, since a tree of rank $r$ has
at least $2^{r}$ nodes.

\smallskip
\textbf{Path compression.} After \algname{Find} locates the root, make every
node on the path point directly at it. The walk is done anyway; flattening
costs one extra pass and makes all future finds on those nodes $\bigO{1}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Together} they give an amortised cost of $\bigO{\alpha(|V|)}$ per
operation, where $\alpha$ is the inverse Ackermann function. $\alpha(n) \le 4$
for every $n$ that can be written down --- $\alpha(n) = 4$ requires $n$ larger
than the number of atoms in the universe.

\smallskip
\textbf{This bound is amortised and nothing else.} A single \algname{Find} can
cost $\bigTh{\lg |V|}$; it is only over a sequence that the average falls to
$\alpha$. It is proved by the potential method of \chref{amortised}, and it is
the most dramatic amortised result in common use.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{theorem}[Kruskal's running time]\label{thm:kruskaltime}
With union by rank and path compression, \algname{Kruskal} runs in
$\bigO{|E| \lg |E|}$ time, which is $\bigO{|E| \lg |V|}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Sorting the edges is $\bigTh{|E|\lg|E|}$. There are $|V|$ \algname{Make-Set}
operations and at most $2|E|$ \algname{Find} plus $|V|-1$ \algname{Union}
operations, costing $\bigO{(|E|+|V|)\,\alpha(|V|)}$ in total. Since
$\alpha(|V|) = \bigO{\lg |E|}$ trivially, the sort dominates, giving
$\bigO{|E|\lg|E|}$. Finally $|E| \le |V|^{2}$ gives $\lg|E| \le 2\lg|V|$, so
the bound is $\bigO{|E|\lg|V|}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{alertbox}[Note what the theorem says about the clever part]
The union--find work is \emph{not} the bottleneck --- the sort is. So why
bother with path compression at all?

\smallskip
Because without it the union--find work \emph{becomes} the bottleneck, by a
long way. Section~19.4 measures a naive implementation running 1{,}254$\times$
slower than the optimised one on the same graph. An optimisation that makes a
non-dominant term stay non-dominant is still doing essential work.
\end{alertbox}

\section{Prim}

\dsfig{\aoaalg{\algname{Prim}$(G, w, r)$}{
\For{each vertex $u$}
  \State $\mathit{key}[u] \gets \infty$;\quad $\pi[u] \gets$ \textsc{nil}
\EndFor
\State $\mathit{key}[r] \gets 0$
\State $Q \gets$ min-priority queue of all vertices, keyed by $\mathit{key}$
\While{$Q$ is not empty}
  \State $u \gets \algname{Extract-Min}(Q)$
    \Comment{the light edge leaving the tree}
  \For{each $v$ adjacent to $u$}
    \If{$v \in Q$ \textbf{and} $w(u,v) < \mathit{key}[v]$}
      \State $\pi[v] \gets u$;\quad
             $\algname{Decrease-Key}(Q, v, w(u,v))$
    \EndIf
  \EndFor
\EndWhile
}}{\algname{Prim}. With a binary heap this is $\bigO{(|V|+|E|)\lg|V|}$; the
cut is always ``tree so far'' against ``everything else''.}{prim}

\begin{conceptbox}[Which heap, and what it is worth]
\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{3.6cm}L{3.2cm}L{3.2cm}L{4.0cm}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
\textbf{Priority queue} & \textbf{Extract-Min} & \textbf{Decrease-Key} &
\textbf{Prim total} \\
\midrule
\endhead
unsorted array & $\bigO{|V|}$ & $\bigO{1}$ & $\bigO{|V|^{2}}$ \\
binary heap & $\bigO{\lg|V|}$ & $\bigO{\lg|V|}$ & $\bigO{|E|\lg|V|}$ \\
Fibonacci heap & $\bigO{\lg|V|}$ & $\bigO{1}$ amortised &
  $\bigO{|E| + |V|\lg|V|}$ \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\smallskip
The Fibonacci heap's $\bigO{1}$ \algname{Decrease-Key} is amortised --- another
debt to \chref{amortised} --- and it gives the best known asymptotic bound.
Its constants are poor enough that binary heaps win in practice for all but
enormous dense graphs, which is a recurring theme: \chref{selection}'s
median-of-medians, \chref{quicksort}'s heapsort fallback, and now this.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Measured}

\begin{worked}[First, Do They Agree?]
Two different greedy rules; the cut property says they must produce the same
total weight.
\begin{center}
\textbf{2{,}000 random connected graphs. Disagreements: 0.}
\end{center}
Cheap evidence that Theorem~\ref{thm:cut} is doing what it claims --- and, more
practically, that neither implementation has a bug.
\end{worked}

64 & 3{,}200{,}000 & 303.1 & 226.4 & Prim \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\smallskip
\textbf{Neither wins everywhere, and the pattern is not monotonic.} Prim wins
at the extreme sparse end and again when dense; Kruskal wins in the middle.

\smallskip
\textbf{Both are $\bigO{|E|\lg|V|}$}, so asymptotic analysis predicts no
winner at all --- and is right, in the sense that the measured ratio never
exceeds about 2 in either direction. Everything in this table is constants.

\smallskip
\textbf{The practical rules that come out of it:}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Kruskal} if the edges are \emph{already sorted}, or can be
        sorted cheaply --- then its dominant term vanishes;
  \item \textbf{Prim} on dense graphs, where it never materialises an edge
        list at all and touches the adjacency structure in place;
  \item \textbf{either}, in the broad middle, where the difference is under
        2$\times$ and other considerations should decide.
\end{tight}
\end{worked}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Union--find without union by rank.} Measured 14{,}000$\times$
        more pointer hops and 44 seconds against 35 milliseconds.
  \item \textbf{Quoting $\bigO{\alpha(n)}$ as a worst case.} It is amortised; a
        single find can still be $\bigTh{\lg|V|}$.
  \item \textbf{Assuming the MST is unique.} It is unique only if all edge
        weights are distinct. With ties there may be many, and two correct
        algorithms may return different trees of the same weight.
  \item \textbf{Running an MST algorithm on a disconnected graph.} There is no
        spanning tree; you get a spanning \emph{forest}, and the code should
        say which it computed.
  \item \textbf{Using Kruskal on a dense graph without thinking about the
        sort.} $\bigTh{|E|\lg|E|}$ with $|E| = \bigTh{|V|^{2}}$ is
        $\bigTh{|V|^{2}\lg|V|}$.
  \item \textbf{Reaching for a Fibonacci heap.} Better asymptotically, worse in
        practice at every size you will meet --- the same story as
        median-of-medians.
  \item \textbf{Forgetting that the cut property needs $A$ to cross no edge of
        the cut.} That hypothesis is what makes the exchange safe.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lab}[Where Amortised Analysis Pays Off]
Reproduces all four tables. Expect forty minutes --- the naive union--find at
$|V| = 100{,}000$ takes 44 seconds per run, three times over.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++]
// ch19_mst.cpp -- Kruskal, Prim, and where Chapter 17 pays off.
// Build: cl /O2 /EHsc /std:c++17 ch19_mst.cpp        (see Appendix A)

// --- 1. Union-find, three ways -------------------------------------
// The only differences are whether union balances the trees and
// whether find flattens the path behind it. Both are two lines, and
// together they take the amortised cost from O(lg n) to O(alpha(n)).
    int find(int x) {
        if (!compress) {
            while (parent[x] != x) { x = parent[x]; ++steps; }
            return x;
        }
        int root = x;
        while (parent[root] != root) { root = parent[root]; ++steps; }
        while (parent[x] != root) {          // second pass: flatten
            int next = parent[x];
            parent[x] = root;
            x = next;
        }
        return root;
    }
    bool unite(int a, int b) {
        a = find(a); b = find(b);
        if (a == b) return false;
        if (!by_rank) { parent[a] = b; return true; }
        if (rank_[a] < rank_[b]) std::swap(a, b);
        parent[b] = a;
        if (rank_[a] == rank_[b]) ++rank_[a];
        return true;
    }

// --- 2. Kruskal: sort, then add anything that is safe --------------
// The sort is Theta(E lg E) and dominates; the union-find work is
// E * alpha(V), which is why the structure's amortised cost matters.

// --- 3. Prim: grow one tree from a root ----------------------------
// The "lazy" form pushes every edge and discards stale entries when
// popped, which is simpler than decrease-key and no worse
// asymptotically.

// --- 4. Do the two algorithms agree? -------------------------------
// They are different greedy rules. If the cut property is right, they
// must produce the same total weight.

// --- 5. The three union-find variants ------------------------------
// "steps" counts pointer hops inside find(). This is the measurement
// Chapter 17 predicts: naive grows, rank alone is much better, and
// rank + compression is essentially flat.
//
// The naive variant's find() is LINEAR in the component size, so the
// whole run is quadratic and V = 320,000 does not finish in any
// reasonable time. It stops at 160,000; the other two carry on, and
// that asymmetry is itself the result.

// --- 6. And the same thing in seconds ------------------------------
// --- 7. Kruskal against Prim, by density ---------------------------
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What to notice.} The \texttt{rank+compress} column in part 5 goes from
1.62 to 1.88 while $|V|$ multiplies by 512. If you plotted it you would draw a
horizontal line, and the theory says it is growing --- just slower than any
function you can name.

\smallskip
\textbf{Then try to break it.} Implement path compression \emph{without} union
by rank and measure it. Predict first. It is far better than naive and slightly
worse than the pair --- compression alone gives $\bigO{\lg n}$ amortised, and
it is the combination that gives $\alpha$. Two optimisations that each help and
only together give the famous bound is an unusual and instructive situation.
\end{lab}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item The cut property: a light edge crossing any cut uncrossed by the
        current partial tree is safe. It is proved by an exchange argument, and
        it is why both MST algorithms are greedy and optimal.
  \item Kruskal takes the cut between components (needing union--find); Prim
        takes the cut between the tree and the rest (needing a priority queue).
        One theorem, two data structures.
  \item Union by rank gives $\bigO{\lg|V|}$ per find; adding path compression
        gives $\bigO{\alpha(|V|)}$ amortised, with $\alpha \le 4$ for any
        writable $n$.
  \item Measured: pointer hops per find were 25{,}469 (naive), 3.44 (rank) and
        1.82 (rank + compression) at $|V| = 160{,}000$ --- a ratio of 14{,}009.
  \item Measured: from $|V| = 10^{4}$ to $5.12\times10^{6}$, the optimised cost
        per find rose only from 1.62 to 1.88.
  \item Measured: Kruskal took 44.3 seconds with naive union--find and 35
        milliseconds with the optimisations, at $|V| = 100{,}000$. Rank alone
        got most of the way; compression added a further 20\%.
  \item Measured: Kruskal and Prim never differed by more than about 2$\times$
        across densities, and neither won everywhere. Both are
        $\bigO{|E|\lg|V|}$; the table is entirely constants.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}
