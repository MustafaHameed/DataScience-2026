\chapter{The Boundary With Other Courses}\label{app:boundary}
%=====================================================================
\normalfigures

About a third of the HEC outline for this course is also in the outline for
\emph{Data Structures}, which BSIT students take three or four semesters
earlier. This appendix records what belongs where, so that a lecturer can see
what to assume and a student can see why a familiar topic is being covered
again.

HEC's own ACM mapping (2023, p.~10) groups \emph{Data Structures},
\emph{Analysis of Algorithms} and \emph{Theory of Automata} as jointly
discharging the Algorithms and Complexity knowledge area. The split below is
this handout's reading of that grouping.

\section{Data Structures}

\begin{conceptbox}[The rule]
\textbf{Data Structures owns the structures and their operations. This course
owns their analysis.}

\smallskip
A student arriving here should already be able to insert into a heap, walk a
tree and resolve a hash collision. What this course adds is \emph{why the
heights and the costs are what they are}, and what happens when the assumptions
behind them fail.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{alertbox}[What this course does \emph{not} re-teach]
Pointer manipulation, rotations in a balanced tree, iterator design, memory
management, the implementation of a linked list.

\smallskip
\chref{structures} represents a binary search tree as three parallel arrays
precisely so that no part of it is about pointers: the measurement is about
\emph{shape}, and pointers would be a distraction.
\end{alertbox}

\section{Theory of Automata}

\begin{conceptbox}[The line]
\textbf{Theory of Automata owns computability. This course owns complexity.}

\smallskip
\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{5.2cm}L{4.4cm}L{4.7cm}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
\textbf{Question} & \textbf{Course} & \textbf{Answer type} \\
\midrule
\endhead
Can this be computed at all? & Theory of Automata & decidable / undecidable \\
What is a Turing machine? & Theory of Automata & a model of computation \\
Is the halting problem solvable? & Theory of Automata & no, provably \\
Among computable problems, which are \emph{efficient}? & \textbf{this course} &
  P, NP, NP-complete (\chref{complexity}) \\
Is $\mathrm{P} = \mathrm{NP}$? & \textbf{this course} & open \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\smallskip
\textbf{The distinction is worth making to students explicitly}, because the
two sound alike and are not. The halting problem has \emph{no algorithm}.
NP-complete problems all have algorithms --- \chref{complexity} runs one --- and
the algorithms are merely too slow. Confusing ``undecidable'' with
``intractable'' is the commonest error in this area, and \chref{complexity}'s
pitfall list names it.

\smallskip
\chref{complexity} therefore states the Cook--Levin theorem and uses it, but
does not prove it: the proof is a simulation of a Turing machine, and Turing
machines are the other course's subject.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Discrete Structures}

\begin{conceptbox}[Assumed throughout, re-derived nowhere]
Appendix~B collects what this course uses: logarithm identities, arithmetic and
geometric series, induction, elementary probability, and the handful of
inequalities in Section~B.6.

\smallskip
All of it is \emph{Discrete Structures} material. Appendix~B exists so that a
chapter can point at a result rather than re-prove it, and so that a student
who has forgotten one can find it in two pages rather than in a textbook.

\smallskip
The three items that carry the most weight, and that are worth revising before
Week 4: \textbf{linearity of expectation}, \textbf{$a^{\log_{b}n} =
n^{\log_{b}a}$}, and \textbf{the geometric series}.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Programming Fundamentals and OOP}

\begin{conceptbox}[What is assumed about C++]
The labs assume you can write and compile a C++ program with loops, arrays,
functions and \texttt{std::vector}. They do not assume templates, inheritance,
operator overloading or the finer points of move semantics.

\smallskip
Where a modern facility is used --- a lambda passed to the timing helper, a
structured binding in a range-for --- the lab's commentary says what it does.
Appendix~A gives the compiler setup and the one index-translation trap that
catches people.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{A Note on the 2017 Postgraduate Course}

\begin{conceptbox}[\emph{Advanced Algorithm Analysis}, deleted]
The HEC 2017 curriculum listed a postgraduate elective, \emph{Advanced
Algorithm Analysis}, covering amortised analysis in depth, advanced data
structures (Fibonacci heaps, van Emde Boas trees), network flow, linear
programming, approximation algorithms and randomised algorithms.

\smallskip
\textbf{The 2023 and 2025 editions do not list it}, and no replacement appears.
So the topics below are now covered nowhere in the BSIT programme unless an
individual department adds them:

\begin{tight}
  \item network flow and max-flow/min-cut;
  \item linear programming and duality;
  \item approximation algorithms with proved ratios ---
        \chref{complexity} names them and does not develop them;
  \item advanced randomised algorithms beyond \chref{quicksort}'s pivot and
        \chref{hashing}'s universal families.
\end{tight}

\smallskip
This handout touches three of the four in passing, deliberately:
\chref{amortised} covers amortised analysis properly (the one topic the deleted
course had that this one genuinely needs), \chref{mst} and
\chref{shortestpaths} mention Fibonacci heaps without developing them, and
\chref{complexity} states the three strategies for NP-complete problems.

\smallskip
\textbf{A department wanting to restore the deleted material} would find
\chref{amortised}, \chref{complexity} and the project of \chref{project} the
natural places to extend.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Summary: What to Assume on Day One}

\begin{alertbox}[For the lecturer]
\textbf{Assume} the student can: write and compile C++; implement a linked
list, stack, queue, heap, BST and hash table; write a recursive function; say
informally that a nested loop is ``$\bigO{n^{2}}$''.

\smallskip
\textbf{Do not assume} the student can: state the definition of $\bigO{\cdot}$;
prove anything from it; solve a recurrence; distinguish worst case from
expected case; or say what $\bigO{1}$ amortised means.

\smallskip
\textbf{Revise briefly, do not re-teach.} \chref{structures} spends one chapter
on heaps, trees and the structures beneath, and spends it entirely on analysis.
\chref{hashing} does the same for hash tables. Neither re-implements anything,
and the front matter's prerequisite self-check is there to tell a student
whether that assumption holds for them.
\end{alertbox}
