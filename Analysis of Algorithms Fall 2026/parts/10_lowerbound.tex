\section{A Different Kind of Question}

Every result so far has been about an algorithm: \emph{this} sort takes
$\bigTh{n\lg n}$, \emph{that} one $\bigTh{n^{2}}$. This chapter asks something
else.

\begin{conceptbox}[Algorithms and problems]
\textbf{An upper bound is a statement about an algorithm.} ``Merge sort is
$\bigO{n\lg n}$'' is proved by exhibiting merge sort and analysing it. It says
sorting can be done at least this fast.

\smallskip
\textbf{A lower bound is a statement about a problem.} ``Comparison sorting is
$\bigOm{n\lg n}$'' must hold for \emph{every} comparison sort --- including the
ones nobody has invented yet. It cannot be proved by examining algorithms one
at a time, because there are infinitely many.

\smallskip
That difference is why this chapter is the hinge of the book. Up to here the
subject has been ingenuity; from here it also has limits, and \chref{complexity}
is about the largest open question concerning them.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{The Decision Tree}

\begin{definitionbox}[Comparison Sort]
A \term{comparison sort} determines the order of its input using only
comparisons between pairs of elements. It may ask ``is $a_{i} \le a_{j}$?'' and
move elements about, and it may do nothing else with their values --- no
arithmetic on them, no indexing by them, no inspection of their bits.

\smallskip
Insertion sort, merge sort, quicksort, heapsort and every sort in Part~II are
comparison sorts.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{definitionbox}[Decision Tree]
Fix a comparison sort and an input size $n$. Its \term{decision tree} is a
binary tree in which each internal node is a comparison $a_{i} : a_{j}$, the
two edges are the two outcomes, and each leaf is a permutation --- the answer
the algorithm outputs when it reaches that leaf.

\smallskip
Running the algorithm on an input traces one root-to-leaf path. The number of
comparisons made is the length of that path, and the worst-case number of
comparisons is the \term{height} of the tree.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{theorem}[The comparison sorting lower bound]\label{thm:lowerbound}
Any comparison sort requires $\bigOm{n \lg n}$ comparisons in the worst case.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Consider the decision tree of any comparison sort on $n$ elements, and let $h$
be its height and $\ell$ its number of leaves.

\smallskip
\textbf{Every permutation must appear as a leaf.} There are $n!$ distinct
orderings of $n$ distinct elements. If some ordering labelled no leaf, then on
the input requiring that ordering the algorithm would reach a leaf labelled
with a different permutation and output it --- producing an unsorted array. So
$\ell \ge n!$.

\smallskip
\textbf{A binary tree of height $h$ has at most $2^{h}$ leaves}, by induction
on $h$: a tree of height 0 is a single leaf, and a tree of height $h$ has two
subtrees of height at most $h-1$, hence at most $2 \cdot 2^{h-1} = 2^{h}$
leaves between them.

\smallskip
\textbf{Combining,} $2^{h} \ge \ell \ge n!$, so
\[ h \;\ge\; \lg(n!). \]

\smallskip
\textbf{And $\lg(n!) = \bigOm{n\lg n}$}, by Stirling's approximation
$n! > (n/e)^{n}$:
\[ \lg(n!) \;>\; \lg\Bigl(\frac{n}{e}\Bigr)^{n}
   \;=\; n \lg n - n \lg e
   \;=\; n\lg n - 1.4427\,n \;=\; \bigOm{n \lg n}. \]
Since $h$ is the worst-case number of comparisons, the bound follows.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}
Merge sort and heapsort are asymptotically optimal comparison sorts, and
randomised quicksort is optimal in expectation.
\end{corollary}

\begin{alertbox}[What the theorem does \emph{not} say]
It does not say sorting is $\bigOm{n\lg n}$. It says \emph{comparison} sorting
is.

\smallskip
The proof's entire force comes from the branching factor being 2: each
comparison yields one bit, and distinguishing $n!$ possibilities needs
$\lg(n!)$ bits. An algorithm that extracts more than one bit per operation ---
by using a key's value as an array index, say --- is not modelled by this tree
at all, and Section~10.4 presents two such algorithms. They are not
counter-examples; they are outside the hypothesis.
\end{alertbox}

\section{How Close Does a Real Sort Get?}

The bound says at least $\lg(n!)$ comparisons. Merge sort achieves
$\bigO{n\lg n}$. Those agree asymptotically --- but by what constant does a
real sort miss the floor?

\begin{worked}[The Floor Itself]
\texttt{code/ch10\_lowerbound.cpp} computes $\lg(n!)$ by summing logarithms
(forming $n!$ would overflow at $n = 21$).

\smallskip
\textbf{Stirling's form is essentially exact.} $n\lg n - 1.443n$ differs from
$\lg(n!)$ by 11 out of 18 million at $n = 10^{6}$ --- a relative error of
$6 \times 10^{-7}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{The crude form is not.} $\lg(n!)/(n\lg n)$ is only $0.86$ at
$n = 1{,}000$, creeping to $0.93$ at a million. The $-1.443n$ term matters at
every size you will ever sort, which is why the sharper form is the one to
quote.
\end{worked}

\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{@{}rrrr@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}

\midrule

10{,}000    &     120{,}505 &     118{,}458 & 1.7\% \\
100{,}000   &   1{,}536{,}346 &   1{,}516{,}704 & 1.3\% \\
1{,}000{,}000 & 18{,}672{,}689 & 18{,}488{,}885 & \textbf{1.0\%} \\
\bottomrule

\smallskip
\textbf{Merge sort is within 1\% of the information-theoretic minimum at
$n = 10^{6}$, and the gap is shrinking.}

\smallskip
That is a stronger statement than ``asymptotically optimal''. Asymptotic
optimality permits a constant factor of 10; this is a constant factor of 1.01.
No comparison sort --- none that exists, none that will ever be invented ---
can beat merge sort's comparison count by more than one per cent at this size.

\smallskip
\textbf{Which puts the remaining engineering in its place.} \chref{mergesort}
found \texttt{std::sort} beating merge sort by 39\% in \emph{time}. Since the
comparison count cannot improve by more than 1\%, essentially all of that 39\%
is memory traffic and branch behaviour --- not comparisons. The lower bound
tells you where the remaining performance is \emph{not}.
\end{worked}

\section{Sorting Without Comparing}

To beat $\bigOm{n\lg n}$ you must leave the comparison model. Both ways of
doing so buy linearity with an assumption about what the keys \emph{are}.

\dsfig{\aoaalg{\algname{Counting-Sort}$(A, n, K)$}{
\State let $C[0 \mathinner{\ldotp\ldotp} K-1]$ be a new array of zeros
\For{$i \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $n$}
  \State $C[A[i]] \gets C[A[i]] + 1$
    \Comment{how many of each value}
\EndFor
\For{$v \gets 1$ \textbf{to} $K-1$}
  \State $C[v] \gets C[v] + C[v-1]$
    \Comment{running totals: where each value ends}
\EndFor
\For{$i \gets n$ \textbf{downto} $1$}
  \State $C[A[i]] \gets C[A[i]] - 1$
  \State $B[\,C[A[i]]\,] \gets A[i]$
    \Comment{downwards keeps it stable}
\EndFor
\State \Return $B$
}}{\algname{Counting-Sort}. No element is ever compared with another: line~3
uses a key as an \emph{index}, which the decision-tree model does not permit
and which is why the bound does not apply. Running time $\bigTh{n + K}$.}{counting}

\begin{theorem}[Counting sort]\label{thm:counting}
If every key is an integer in $[0, K)$, \algname{Counting-Sort} sorts $n$ keys
in $\bigTh{n + K}$ time and $\bigTh{n + K}$ space, and is stable.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
The three loops run $n$, $K-1$ and $n$ times respectively with $\bigO{1}$
bodies, giving $\bigTh{n+K}$; the arrays $B$ and $C$ give the space.

\smallskip
For correctness, observe after the second loop that $C[v]$ holds the number of
keys $\le v$. Hence the keys equal to $v$ belong in positions
$C[v-1], \ldots, C[v]-1$ of the output. The third loop places each key $A[i]$
at $C[A[i]] - 1$ and then decrements $C[A[i]]$, so successive keys of the same
value fill that block from its top downwards. Scanning $i$ from $n$ down to 1
therefore places the \emph{last} occurrence highest, preserving input order
among equal keys --- which is stability.
\end{proof}

\begin{alertbox}[Read the hypothesis, which is where the cost went]
$\bigTh{n+K}$ is linear only when $K = \bigO{n}$.

\smallskip
Sorting a million 32-bit integers with counting sort means $K = 2^{32}$ and an
array of four billion counters --- 16 GB, to sort 4 MB of data. The algorithm
is not fast there; it is unusable.

\smallskip
Counting sort is the right choice for ages, exam marks, pixel values, bytes,
days of the year --- small known universes. Radix sort is what lifts the
restriction.
\end{alertbox}

\begin{conceptbox}[Radix sort: counting sort, one digit at a time]
Treat a 32-bit key as four 8-bit digits. Counting-sort the keys by the least
significant digit, then by the next, and so on. Each pass has $K = 256$, so
each is $\bigTh{n + 256} = \bigTh{n}$, and there are 4 passes, giving
$\bigTh{n}$ overall --- more precisely $\bigTh{d(n+R)}$ for $d$ digits of
radix $R$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Stability is not a nicety here; it is the mechanism.} The pass on digit
$i$ must not disturb the relative order established by the passes on digits
$0 \ldots i-1$, or the earlier work is destroyed. This is why
Theorem~\ref{thm:counting} bothered to prove stability, and why the third loop
of \algname{Counting-Sort} counts downwards.

\smallskip
\textbf{And the assumption has not vanished, only changed shape:} from ``keys
come from a small universe'' to ``keys have a fixed width''. Sorting
variable-length strings by radix is a more delicate business.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{worked}[Beating the Bound, Measured]
One million to 6.4 million keys drawn uniformly from $[0, 1000)$, so counting
sort's $K = 1000$ is genuinely small. Times in milliseconds, median of 5.

\smallskip
\textbf{Counting sort is 7--10$\times$ faster than the best comparison sort in
the standard library}, and all three produce identical output.

\smallskip
\textbf{No theorem was violated.} \texttt{std::sort} is bound by
Theorem~\ref{thm:lowerbound}; counting sort is not, because it never compares
two keys. The speed-up is the price the comparison model was charging, made
visible.

\smallskip
\textbf{And the ratio is roughly constant} at about 9, not growing --- because
both algorithms are within a logarithmic factor here and $\lg n$ moves slowly.
The gain is a large constant, not a change of order; at $n = 6.4$ million,
$\lg n = 22.6$, and counting sort recovers about 40\% of that.
\end{worked}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Stating the bound as ``sorting is $\bigOm{n\lg n}$''.} It is
        \emph{comparison} sorting. Dropping the qualifier makes
        Section~10.4 look like a contradiction.
  \item \textbf{Thinking counting sort breaks the theorem.} It is outside the
        hypothesis, not a counter-example to the conclusion.
  \item \textbf{Forgetting the $+K$.} Counting sort on 32-bit keys needs a
        four-billion-entry array.
  \item \textbf{An unstable counting sort inside a radix sort.} The radix
        passes then destroy each other's work and the output is wrong, not
        merely slow.
  \item \textbf{Quoting $\lg(n!) \approx n \lg n$ at small $n$.} At
        $n = 1{,}000$ the ratio is 0.86. Use $n\lg n - 1.443n$.
  \item \textbf{Hunting for a faster comparison sort.} Merge sort is within 1\%
        of the floor at $n = 10^{6}$; the remaining performance is in memory
        behaviour, not comparisons.
  \item \textbf{Assuming linear-time sorts are always faster.} They were 9×
        faster here on a 1000-value universe. Widen the universe and counting
        sort loses, then becomes impossible.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lab}[The Floor, and Going Under It]
Reproduces all three tables. Expect twenty-five minutes.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++]
// ch10_lowerbound.cpp -- the comparison lower bound, and sorting past it.
// Build: cl /O2 /EHsc /std:c++17 ch10_lowerbound.cpp  (see Appendix A)

// --- 1. lg(n!) exactly, by summing logs ----------------------------
// n! overflows at n = 21, so the factorial is never formed: the log of
// a product is the sum of the logs, and that stays in range for ever.
static double lg_factorial(long long n) {
    double s = 0;
    for (long long k = 2; k <= n; ++k) s += std::log2((double)k);
    return s;
}

// --- 2. A comparison sort, counting every comparison ---------------
// Merge sort, because Chapter 8 proved it Theta(n lg n) in every case.
// The question here is how close its CONSTANT is to the floor.

// --- 3. Counting sort: no comparisons at all -----------------------
// It never asks "is x < y". It asks "how many keys equal v", which is
// a different question and not covered by the theorem. The price is
// the assumption that keys are integers in a known, small range.
static void counting_sort(const std::vector<int>& in,
                          std::vector<int>& out, int K) {
    std::vector<int> cnt(K, 0);
    for (int x : in) ++cnt[x];
    for (int v = 1; v < K; ++v) cnt[v] += cnt[v - 1];
    for (size_t i = in.size(); i-- > 0;) out[--cnt[in[i]]] = in[i];
}

// --- 4. Radix sort: counting sort, one digit at a time -------------
// Lifts counting sort's "small range" assumption to "fixed width":
// 32-bit keys in four passes of an 8-bit counting sort. Still no
// comparisons, so still outside the theorem.
static void radix_sort(std::vector<int>& a, std::vector<int>& tmp) {
    const int BITS = 8, R = 1 << BITS;
    size_t n = a.size();
    for (int shift = 0; shift < 32; shift += BITS) {
        std::vector<size_t> cnt(R + 1, 0);
        for (size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
            ++cnt[((unsigned)a[i] >> shift & (R - 1)) + 1];
        for (int v = 0; v < R; ++v) cnt[v + 1] += cnt[v];
        for (size_t i = 0; i < n; ++i)
            tmp[cnt[(unsigned)a[i] >> shift & (R - 1)]++] = a[i];
        a.swap(tmp);
    }
}

// --- 5. The bound itself, and Stirling's sharper form --------------
// lg(n!) >= n lg n - n lg e = n lg n - 1.4427n.

// --- 6. How close does a real sort get to the floor? ---------------
// Merge sort is Theta(n lg n); so is the bound. The question is the
// constant, and the answer is "within a few per cent".

// --- 7. Beating the bound, by not comparing ------------------------
// Counting and radix sort are not counter-examples to the theorem.
// They are outside it: they never compare two keys. What they pay
// instead is an assumption about what the keys ARE.

// --- 8. And they really did sort -----------------------------------
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What to notice.} Part 6's excess column \emph{falls} --- 2.0\%, 1.7\%,
1.3\%, 1.0\%. Merge sort is not merely within a constant factor of optimal; it
is converging to optimal. There is nothing left to win in comparisons.

\smallskip
\textbf{Then try to break it.} Change \texttt{K} from 1000 to 10{,}000{,}000
and re-run part 7. Counting sort's time should barely move for small $n$ and
then be dominated entirely by the $+K$ term --- at $n = 100{,}000$ it will be
\emph{slower} than \texttt{std::sort}, because it is allocating and sweeping an
array a hundred times longer than its input. The $+K$ is not a footnote.
\end{lab}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item A lower bound is a statement about a \emph{problem} and must hold for
        every algorithm, including undiscovered ones. This is the first such
        result in the book.
  \item A comparison sort's decision tree must have $n!$ leaves; a binary tree
        of height $h$ has at most $2^{h}$; hence $h \ge \lg(n!) =
        \bigOm{n\lg n}$.
  \item Merge sort, heapsort and randomised quicksort are therefore
        asymptotically optimal comparison sorts.
  \item Measured: $\lg(n!)$ matches $n\lg n - 1.443n$ to a relative error of
        $6\times10^{-7}$, while the crude $n \lg n$ is 14\% high at
        $n = 1{,}000$.
  \item Measured: merge sort uses 1.0\% more comparisons than the floor at
        $n = 10^{6}$, and the excess is falling. No comparison sort can do
        appreciably better.
  \item Counting sort ($\bigTh{n+K}$) and radix sort ($\bigTh{d(n+R)}$) are
        linear because they never compare keys --- they index by them. Each
        buys that with an assumption: a small universe, or a fixed width.
  \item Measured: counting sort beat \texttt{std::sort} by 7--10$\times$ on a
        1000-value universe, with identical output and no theorem harmed.
  \item Radix sort requires its per-digit pass to be stable, or earlier passes
        are destroyed.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}
