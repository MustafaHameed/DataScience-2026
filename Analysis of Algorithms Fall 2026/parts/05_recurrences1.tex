\section{What a Recurrence Is}

A recursive algorithm's cost is defined in terms of itself, so its running time
is too.

\begin{definitionbox}[Recurrence]
A \term{recurrence} is an equation that defines a function in terms of its own
value at smaller arguments, together with one or more \term{base cases}.

\smallskip
Merge sort divides its input in half, sorts each half recursively, and merges
the results in linear time. So
\[ T(n) = \begin{cases}
   \Theta(1) & n = 1, \\
   T(\lceil n/2 \rceil) + T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) + \Theta(n) & n > 1.
   \end{cases} \]
Written as algorithm designers actually write it, with the floors dropped and
the constants absorbed: $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n$.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{alertbox}[Three things that are quietly being ignored]
\begin{tightnum}
  \item \textbf{Floors and ceilings.} $n/2$ is not an integer when $n$ is odd.
        Section~5.5 says why dropping them is safe.
  \item \textbf{The base case.} $T(1) = \Theta(1)$ is assumed. For every
        recurrence in this book the base case contributes a constant, which the
        asymptotics absorb.
  \item \textbf{The constant on $n$.} ``$+\,n$'' means $+\,\Theta(n)$. We are
        solving for an order of growth, so a constant factor on the combining
        step cannot change the answer --- though \chref{mergesort} measures
        what it does to the clock.
\end{tightnum}
\end{alertbox}

\section{The Substitution Method}

The substitution method has two steps and no magic in the second one:

\begin{tightnum}
  \item \textbf{Guess} the form of the answer.
  \item \textbf{Prove} it by induction, finding the constants that make the
        induction go through.
\end{tightnum}

The guess is where the work is; Section~5.3 is a machine for producing guesses.

\begin{theorem}[Merge sort's recurrence]\label{thm:mergerec}
If $T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n$ with $T(1) = 1$, then $T(n) = \bigO{n \lg n}$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Guess $T(n) \le cn\lg n$ for a constant $c > 0$ to be chosen, and all
$n \ge n_{0}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Inductive step.} Assume the bound holds for $n/2$, that is
$T(n/2) \le c\,(n/2)\lg(n/2)$. Then
\begin{align*}
T(n) &= 2T(n/2) + n \\
     &\le 2 \Bigl( c\,\frac{n}{2}\lg\frac{n}{2} \Bigr) + n
      = cn\lg\frac{n}{2} + n \\
     &= cn\lg n - cn\lg 2 + n
      = cn\lg n - cn + n.
\end{align*}
This is at most $cn \lg n$ provided $-cn + n \le 0$, that is $c \ge 1$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Base case.} The induction needs some $n$ at which the bound holds
outright. At $n = 1$ it does not: $T(1) = 1$ but $c \cdot 1 \cdot \lg 1 = 0$.
This is not a flaw in the result --- asymptotic bounds need hold only for
$n \ge n_{0}$, so take $n_{0} = 2$. Then $T(2) = 2T(1) + 2 = 4$, and
$c \cdot 2 \lg 2 = 2c \ge 4$ whenever $c \ge 2$.

\smallskip
Taking $c = 2$ and $n_{0} = 2$ satisfies both conditions, so
$T(n) \le 2n\lg n$ for all $n \ge 2$, and $T(n) = \bigO{n\lg n}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{alertbox}[The base case is where students lose marks, not the algebra]
Notice that the inductive step needed only $c \ge 1$, and the base case forced
$c \ge 2$. Both constraints are real and the proof must satisfy both.

\smallskip
Notice also that $n = 1$ \emph{could not} be made to work and did not have to
be. Moving the base case up to $n = 2$ is legitimate precisely because
$\bigO{\cdot}$ quantifies over $n \ge n_{0}$ --- the definition in
\chref{asymptotics} was built for exactly this.
\end{alertbox}

\begin{worked}[A Guess That Fails, and the Repair]
\textbf{Claim to test:} $T(n) = T(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor) +
T(\lceil n/2 \rceil) + 1$ is $\bigO{n}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- the obvious attempt.} Guess $T(n) \le cn$. Then
\[ T(n) \le c\Bigl\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \Bigr\rfloor
          + c\Bigl\lceil \frac{n}{2} \Bigr\rceil + 1 = cn + 1. \]

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 2 --- why that is a failure.} $cn + 1$ is \emph{not} $\le cn$, for
any $c$. The algebra came out ``nearly'' right, and nearly is no good: the
induction does not close, so nothing has been proved. No choice of $c$ repairs
it, because the leftover $+1$ does not involve $c$ at all.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 3 --- strengthen the hypothesis.} The trick is to prove something
\emph{stronger}, which gives the induction more to work with. Guess
$T(n) \le cn - d$ for constants $c, d > 0$:
\begin{align*}
T(n) &\le \Bigl(c\Bigl\lfloor \tfrac{n}{2} \Bigr\rfloor - d\Bigr)
        + \Bigl(c\Bigl\lceil \tfrac{n}{2} \Bigr\rceil - d\Bigr) + 1 \\
     &= cn - 2d + 1 \;\le\; cn - d \quad\text{whenever } d \ge 1.
\end{align*}

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 4 --- the lesson.} Subtracting a lower-order term made the proof
work. It looks backwards --- proving more should be harder --- but the
inductive hypothesis is \emph{used} as well as proved, and a stronger
hypothesis is a stronger tool. This is the single most useful trick in the
method, and the one worth remembering.
\end{worked}

\section{Recursion Trees: a Way to Guess}

the work that call does \emph{excluding} its recursive calls; the total is the
sum over all nodes, which is easiest taken level by level.

\begin{conceptbox}[A tree is a guess, not a proof]
The tree gives $\bigTh{n\lg n}$ in two lines. It is not a proof: the levels
were assumed uniform, the leaves were counted loosely, and floors were ignored
throughout.

\smallskip
That is the correct division of labour. \textbf{Draw the tree to get the
guess; run substitution to prove it.} Theorem~\ref{thm:mergerec} is the proof
for exactly the guess this tree produced.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{An Unbalanced Split}

Real algorithms do not always divide evenly. Quicksort (\chref{quicksort})
splits wherever the pivot lands. What does a lopsided split cost?

\begin{worked}[$T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + n$]
\textbf{Step 1 --- the shallowest leaf.} Following the $n/3$ branch, the
argument is divided by 3 each time, reaching 1 after $\log_{3} n$ steps.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 2 --- the deepest leaf.} Following the $2n/3$ branch, the argument
is multiplied by $2/3$ each time, reaching 1 after $\log_{3/2} n$ steps.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 3 --- what that means for the sum.} While \emph{every} branch is
still alive --- down to depth $\log_{3} n$ --- each level totals exactly $n$,
because the two children's arguments sum to the parent's. Below that, branches
start bottoming out and the levels total less than $n$. So
\[ \underbrace{n \log_{3} n}_{\text{full levels}}
   \;\le\; T(n) \;\le\; \underbrace{n \log_{3/2} n}_{\text{if every level were full}}, \]
and since $\log_{3} n$ and $\log_{3/2} n$ are both $\bigTh{\lg n}$ --- they
differ from $\lg n$ only by the constants $1/\lg 3$ and $1/\lg(3/2)$ --- we get
$T(n) = \bigTh{n \lg n}$.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 4 --- the moral.} \emph{Any} split into constant fractions gives
$\bigTh{n\lg n}$. A 1-to-99 split is still $\bigTh{n\lg n}$; only the constant
changes. What breaks the bound is a split that is not a constant fraction ---
such as $1$ and $n-1$, which is \chref{quicksort}'s worst case.
\end{worked}

\section{Floors, Ceilings and Boundaries}

\begin{conceptbox}[Why dropping them is safe]
Every recurrence above was written $T(n/2)$ when the honest version is
$T(\lfloor n/2\rfloor) + T(\lceil n/2 \rceil)$.

\smallskip
\textbf{The justification is a sandwich.} For the recurrences in this book,
\[ T\bigl(\lfloor n/2 \rfloor\bigr) \;\le\; T(n/2) \;\le\;
   T\bigl(\lceil n/2 \rceil\bigr), \]
since $T$ is non-decreasing, and the outer two differ by at most one step of
the recursion --- a $\bigO{n}$ term already present. Carrying the floors
through a substitution proof changes the constants and never the order of
growth, which is why textbooks state the clean form and prove the messy one
once.

\smallskip
\textbf{The same goes for the base case.} $T(1) = 1$ against $T(1) = 1000$
changes $c$, not the bound: the base-case work is $\bigO{1}$ per leaf and
there are $\bigO{n}$ leaves, contributing $\bigO{n}$, which $n\lg n$ absorbs.

\smallskip
\textbf{Where this stops being true} is a recurrence whose subproblem sizes do
not shrink by a constant fraction --- $T(n) = T(n-1) + n$, say, whose tree is a
path of length $n$ and whose solution is $\bigTh{n^{2}}$. There the ``$-1$''
is the whole content and may not be tidied away.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Guessing from the tree and calling it proved.} The tree
        produces a guess. Substitution proves it. Both steps are required.
  \item \textbf{An inductive step that comes out ``nearly'' right.}
        $cn + 1 \le cn$ is false, and a proof that ends there has proved
        nothing. Strengthen the hypothesis.
  \item \textbf{Forgetting the base case.} The inductive step above needed
        $c \ge 1$ and the base case needed $c \ge 2$; a proof that checks only
        the step is half a proof.
  \item \textbf{Insisting the base case be $n = 1$.} It need not be. $\bigO{}$
        quantifies over $n \ge n_{0}$ and you may choose $n_{0}$.
  \item \textbf{Assuming every level of a tree is full.} It is true for a
        balanced split and false for a lopsided one from depth
        $\log_{b_{\min}} n$ onward --- as the measured table shows, to the
        level.
  \item \textbf{Dropping a $-1$ as though it were a $/2$.}
        $T(n) = T(n-1)+n$ is quadratic, not logarithmic-depth anything.
  \item \textbf{Proving $\bigO{}$ and claiming $\bigTh{}$.} Substitution as
        used here gives an upper bound. The lower bound is a separate proof,
        by Theorem~3.5.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lab}[Counting a Recursion Tree]
Reproduces all three tables above. Expect twenty minutes.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=C++]
// ch05_rectree.cpp -- a recursion tree, counted rather than drawn.
// Build: cl /O2 /EHsc /std:c++17 ch05_rectree.cpp   (see Appendix A)

// --- 1. The balanced recursion, T(n) = 2T(n/2) + n -----------------
// The body does n units of work and then halves. Nothing is sorted
// here: the point is the SHAPE, so work is counted, not performed.
static void balanced(long long n, int d) {
    if (n <= 1) { note(d, 1); return; }
    note(d, n);
    balanced(n / 2, d + 1);
    balanced(n - n / 2, d + 1);
}

// --- 2. The lopsided recursion, T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + n --------
// The max(1, ...) is not decoration. Written as lopsided(n/3) and
// lopsided(n - n/3), the case n = 2 gives 2/3 = 0 and 2 - 0 = 2, so
// the larger branch recurses on 2 for ever: the measure n never
// decreases and the recursion does not terminate. That is Chapter 4's
// third obligation, failing in a program four lines long.
static void lopsided(long long n, int d) {
    if (n <= 1) { note(d, 1); return; }
    note(d, n);
    long long small = std::max(1LL, n / 3);
    lopsided(small, d + 1);
    lopsided(n - small, d + 1);
}

// --- 3. Each level of a balanced tree does the same work -----------
// The "work/n" column is the one to read: if the recursion-tree
// argument is sound, it is 1.000 all the way down to the leaves.

// --- 4. A lopsided tree, where the levels stop being full ----------
// Now "work/n" holds at 1.000 only while every branch is still alive
// -- down to depth log_3(n) -- and then decays.

// --- 5. Does the depth grow the way the algebra says? --------------
// Balanced depth should be lg(n); lopsided depth log_{3/2}(n), about
// 1.71 times as deep.
for (long long n = 1024; n <= 262144; n *= 4) {
    work.assign(1, 0); calls.assign(1, 0);
    balanced(n, 0);
    size_t db = work.size();
    work.assign(1, 0); calls.assign(1, 0);
    lopsided(n, 0);
    printf("%10lld %8zu %8.1f %8zu %8.1f %8.2f\n",
           n, db, std::log2((double)n), work.size(),
           std::log2((double)n) / std::log2(1.5),
           (double)work.size() / db);
}
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What to notice.} The first version of the \texttt{lopsided} function
written for this book had no \texttt{max(1, ...)} and did not terminate. It is
worth pausing on that: the recurrence $T(n) = T(n/3) + T(2n/3) + n$ is
perfectly well defined, and the obvious transcription of it into code is not.
Integer division did it.

\smallskip
\textbf{Then try to break it.} Change the split to $n/10$ and $9n/10$ and
predict, before running, (a) how many levels are full, (b) the total depth, and
(c) whether the total work goes up or down. The answers are $\log_{10} n$,
$\log_{10/9} n$, and up --- but only by a constant.
\end{lab}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item A recurrence defines a running time in terms of itself, plus a base
        case. Floors, ceilings and base constants are dropped because they
        change constants, not orders of growth.
  \item Substitution = guess + induction. The inductive step and the base case
        each constrain the constant, and both constraints must be met.
  \item When an inductive step comes out ``nearly'' right --- $cn+1 \le cn$ ---
        strengthen the hypothesis by subtracting a lower-order term.
  \item A recursion tree is a device for \emph{guessing}: sum level by level,
        multiply work-per-level by number of levels.
  \item Measured: for $T(n)=2T(n/2)+n$ at $n=1024$, every level does exactly
        1024 units, there are exactly 11 levels, and the total is exactly
        $11n$.
  \item Any split into constant fractions is $\bigTh{n\lg n}$. Measured, a
        one-third/two-thirds split was 70\% deeper (ratio 1.68 against a
        predicted 1.71) and did only 10\% more work.
  \item Measured, the lopsided tree's levels were exactly full down to depth 6,
        and $\log_{3} 1024 = 6.29$.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}
