\ansch{1 --- What E-Commerce Is, and What It Changed}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Electronic business, not electronic commerce.} Viewing results
        and downloading transcripts are business processes conducted over a
        network, but no value changes hands through the system --- the
        transaction happens at a bank counter. The moment fee payment moves
        online it becomes e-commerce, and the university acquires every problem
        to the right of the dashed line in \dsref{fourfunctions}: a payment
        that may have succeeded while the network dropped, a receipt that must
        exist exactly once, a refund path.
  \item Many are acceptable. One good answer: \textbf{refusing to sell the last
        item when it is visibly damaged.} In software this requires a stock
        record that distinguishes ``held'' from ``sellable'', somebody whose
        job is to update it, a rule for what the storefront does when the two
        disagree, and a decision about what the customer sees --- out of stock,
        or a quiet substitution? The shopkeeper does all of that in half a
        second without recording that a decision was made, which is exactly why
        it is forgotten when the shop goes online.
  \item \textbf{Two uncounted costs:} the acquisition cost of finding customers
        who currently arrive free (\chref{models} puts this at Rs.~1{,}200 an
        order for a typical small shop, which can exceed the commission), and
        the labour of running the four functions themselves --- payment
        integration, delivery arrangements, customer service, returns. The
        commission was buying those, not just the listing. \textbf{What they
        gain that is not money:} the customer relationship. They learn who
        bought, can contact them again, and cannot be delisted. For a business
        with repeat custom that is frequently worth more than the 15\%.
\end{ans}

\ansch{2 --- Business Models and Revenue Models}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Business model:} a marketplace connecting restaurants with
        diners, with fulfilment performed in-house --- three and a half of the
        four functions. \textbf{Revenue models: three at once} ---
        \emph{commission} from restaurants, a \emph{service or delivery fee}
        from customers, and \emph{advertising} in the form of promoted
        placement. \textbf{Most profitable per unit: the advertising.} It has
        almost no marginal cost, where commission is earned against riders and
        support that must be paid for, and the delivery fee rarely covers the
        rider. Promoted placement is close to pure margin, which is why these
        platforms push it so hard.
  \item Viable when $\text{LTV} > \text{CAC}$, that is when
        $400 \times n > 1{,}500$, so when the average customer orders
        $n > 3.75$ --- \textbf{at least four times} --- before they stop.
        \emph{What to measure:} the repeat rate and the time over which it
        happens. Four orders over six months is a business; four orders over
        six years is not, because the cash was spent today and returns over a
        period during which the venture has to survive.
  \item B2C platforms store \textbf{one price per product} --- a column on the
        product row. B2B pricing is a \emph{relation} between customer and
        product, often with quantity breaks and expiry dates. That is not a
        setting you have failed to find; it is a different cardinality, and
        making it work means either a different platform or an extension that
        intercepts every price lookup in the system (\chref{modules} shows how
        much work that is). The assumption that breaks is ``a product has
        \emph{a} price''.
\end{ans}

\ansch{3 --- Auctions, Portals and Communities}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Bid Rs.~15{,}000} --- your true valuation. You win, and you pay
        the second-highest bid, \textbf{Rs.~13{,}500}, gaining Rs.~1{,}500.
        Bidding Rs.~18{,}000 does not improve this outcome at all: you still
        pay Rs.~13{,}500, because the price is set by the others. What it does
        is expose you to winning when somebody bids Rs.~16{,}500, at which
        point you pay Rs.~16{,}500 for a thing worth Rs.~15{,}000 to you. The
        overbid can only ever buy you an auction you did not want.
  \item It \textbf{never closes}. The auction stays open indefinitely; the
        winning bidder is never notified, the seller is never paid, and the
        listing accumulates further bids whenever somebody wanders past. The
        seller sees an item that appears to be permanently ``ending soon'' and
        a buyer who has given up. Worse, the failure is \emph{silent} and
        inversely proportional to interest: popular auctions close correctly
        and unpopular ones do not, so the bug is invisible in testing, where
        you load every page you create.
  \item Any two of: \textbf{be a seller yourself} until real ones arrive
        (cost: you take on stock and inventory risk, and you compete with the
        sellers you are recruiting); \textbf{narrow the market} to one city or
        one category (cost: you cap your growth and may pick the wrong niche);
        \textbf{give sellers a tool worth having without buyers} (cost: you are
        now building and supporting a second product); \textbf{list supply
        gathered elsewhere} (cost: legally risky and reputationally
        expensive if discovered).
\end{ans}

\ansch{4 --- Planning an E-Commerce Framework}
\begin{ans}
  \item (a)~\textbf{Functional}, and it \emph{does not} discriminate --- all
        four options reset passwords. (b)~\textbf{Functional}, and it
        \textbf{discriminates sharply}: per-customer pricing eliminates most
        hosted platforms and the marketplace route outright, and is the single
        most common reason to choose self-hosted or bespoke. (c)~\textbf{
        Non-functional}, and it discriminates \emph{in the opposite direction}
        to what students expect --- surviving a twentyfold spike is something a
        large hosted platform does effortlessly and your single virtual server
        does not.
  \item \textbf{Hosting} (Rs.~324{,}000 in the worked example),
        \textbf{setup and theming labour} (Rs.~175{,}000 plus
        Rs.~100{,}000 of custom modules), and above all \textbf{maintenance
        labour} (Rs.~360{,}000). Their stated saving of Rs.~792{,}000 is
        therefore closer to Rs.~339{,}000 --- still a saving, but less than
        half what they claimed, and it vanishes entirely if maintenance takes
        eight hours a month instead of four.
  \item Many are acceptable. A clear one: \textbf{a home baker selling six
        kinds of cake to one city, taking orders through Instagram.} Self-hosting
        would mean paying for a server and maintaining it to serve perhaps
        thirty orders a week, with nobody to patch it --- which is not a
        cheaper shop but an unpatched one. \textbf{Choose marketplace-only or a
        cheap hosted platform}: the business's scarce resource is the baker's
        time, the volume is low, nothing about the pricing is unusual, and
        every hour spent on a server is an hour not spent baking.
\end{ans}

\ansch{5 --- The Stack, and Your Shop in One Command}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Working:} the operating system and the web server --- a 500 is
        generated and served by Apache, so the request reached it and a
        response came back. \textbf{Failed:} the application layer; PHP raised
        an error and died before producing a page. The database is
        \emph{unknown} --- it may be the cause (the application could not
        connect) or entirely healthy. \textbf{Look first} at the PHP error log,
        with \texttt{docker compose logs shop}; the message is never on the
        screen, because \texttt{display\_errors} is off, and \chref{security}
        is about why you want it that way.
  \item \texttt{depends\_on} orders container \emph{starts}, and a container
        that has started is not a database that will accept a login --- MariaDB
        opens its TCP port several seconds before it finishes initialising. The
        shop therefore starts, runs the installer, and the installer is refused
        by a database that is listening but not ready. \textbf{The failure is
        intermittent and worse on a fast machine}, because the faster the shop
        container starts, the more certainly it arrives early. The
        \texttt{service\_healthy} condition waits for the database's own
        healthcheck to pass, which tests a login rather than a port.
  \item \textbf{Lost:} both named volumes --- the entire database (products,
        categories, orders, customers, settings) and the shop's installed
        files, including any module you had written in place. \textbf{Not
        lost:} anything in the repository --- the Compose file, the Dockerfile,
        \texttt{code/}, and any module kept there rather than edited inside the
        container. \textbf{What would have made it minor:} a database dump
        taken on a schedule and kept outside the volume, plus the discipline of
        writing modules in \texttt{code/modules/} and mounting them, so that
        the only thing destroyed is state you can regenerate. \chref{deploy}
        makes both of these routine.
\end{ans}

\ansch{6 --- Products, Categories and Their Tables}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Move to \texttt{oc\_product\_description}:} the product name
        and the meta description. Both are \emph{words}, and words differ by
        language. \textbf{Stay in \texttt{oc\_product}:} price, weight and
        stock quantity --- a kilogram is a kilogram in Urdu, and a shop that
        let the price depend on the reader's language would eventually charge
        two different prices for one product. The test is the first of the four
        questions: \emph{does this fact depend on who is reading?}
  \item \textbf{Buys:} a single-query answer to ``every category beneath this
        one'', so a category page costs one query instead of a loop whose
        length depends on how deep the tree happens to be.
        \textbf{Costs:} the same fact is now stored twice, so the two can
        disagree, and every write that changes the shape of the tree must
        rewrite the derived rows. \textbf{The operation that makes it visible:}
        \emph{moving a category}. Changing one \texttt{parent\_id} invalidates
        the path rows of that category and of everything beneath it, so the
        admin panel must rewrite them all --- which is why moving a category
        with many descendants is noticeably slower than renaming one.
  \item Two of several good answers. \textbf{(a)~The database was never the
        bottleneck}: if the database is 20\% of the page, halving it improves
        the page by 10\%, which can be smaller than the run-to-run variance.
        \textbf{(b)~The expensive thing is the \emph{number} of queries, not
        the cost of any one} --- the measurement in this chapter found 146
        queries for one page, and an index helps exactly one of them.
        \smallskip
        \textbf{To tell them apart:} turn on the query log with
        \texttt{long\_query\_time=0}, request the page once, and read
        \texttt{COUNT(*)} and \texttt{SUM(query\_time)} from
        \texttt{mysql.slow\_log}. A large total with few queries is (a); a
        small total spread over a hundred queries is (b). That single
        measurement separates them, and it is the one in
        \texttt{m06\_catalogue.py}.
\end{ans}

\ansch{7 --- Product Variations, Options and Uploads}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{The photograph is an option} --- specifically a \texttt{file}
        option. It is supplied by the customer, it is different every time, and
        the shop holds no stock of it. \textbf{The size is a variant}, because
        a small mug and a large mug are two different objects on two different
        shelves, each with its own cost, its own barcode and its own count.
        \textbf{What the shop holds in stock:} blank mugs in two sizes. Nothing
        else --- which is exactly why the photograph cannot be a variant: there
        is no such thing as stock of ``the mug with Ahmed's photograph on it''
        until Ahmed orders one.
  \item \textbf{Option value rows: $4 + 3 + 2 = \mathbf{9}$}, plus three rows
        in \texttt{oc\_product\_option}. \textbf{As variants: $4 \times 3 \times
        2 = 24$ combinations, so \textbf{25} \texttt{oc\_product} rows} ---
        twenty-four plus the master --- and 25 description rows, 25 store rows
        and 25 category rows beside them. Nine against a hundred, for the same
        catalogue.
  \item \textbf{Passes:} the filename sanitiser (nothing illegal in
        ``logo.svg''), the length check, the extension allow-list
        (\texttt{svg} is on the stock list), and the MIME allow-list --- it
        claimed \texttt{image/png}, which is allowed, and nothing verifies that
        claim. \textbf{Fails:} nothing. All five checks pass.
        \smallskip
        \textbf{For it to harm a visitor}, the file must be \emph{served to
        them from your origin and rendered}. Two things would have to be true:
        the attacker must learn the stored URL, which carries a 32-character
        random token and so is not guessable --- but it \emph{is} shown to
        whoever uploaded it and may be forwarded to an administrator who opens
        it --- and the server must serve it with
        \texttt{Content-Type: image/svg+xml} rather than as a download, which
        \chref{stack}'s stock install does. The realistic victim is therefore
        the administrator reviewing the order, and the realistic fix is to drop
        \texttt{svg} from the list and serve uploads with
        \texttt{Content-Disposition: attachment} (\chref{security}).
\end{ans}

\ansch{8 --- Themes, Layout and the Customer's Experience}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Best case for compressing the photographs:} the images are
        $713 - 597 - 54 = $ about \textbf{61\,KB}. Halving them saves 30\,KB,
        which is \textbf{4\%} of the page --- and that is the \emph{best} case,
        because you cannot compress them to nothing. \textbf{Propose instead:}
        minify \texttt{bootstrap.css}, which is 265\,KB of the 597 and has no
        minified copy in the tree. That single change saves roughly 240\,KB,
        about \textbf{34\%} of the page --- eight times the whole image budget
        --- and changes nothing a customer can see. Then remove the unused
        parts of Bootstrap and FontAwesome, and only then look at the images.
  \item \textbf{Layout change} if a ``trust badge'' module already exists:
        \adminpath{Design, Layouts, Product}, add the module to
        \uiel{Content Bottom}, set its sort order. No code, visible to the next
        person, survives upgrades. \textbf{Template override} if it must sit
        \emph{immediately under the button}, inside the markup the theme
        generates: there is no position there, so you override
        \texttt{product/product.twig} in your child theme and add it. The
        general rule: a position that exists is a layout change; markup that
        must differ is a template change.
  \item \textbf{Two reasons it is lower.} (a)~\textbf{It follows only assets
        named in the HTML.} Fonts are requested by the CSS once the browser
        parses it, so none of the FontAwesome \texttt{.woff2} files --- 248\,KB
        in that directory --- are counted. (b)~\textbf{It does not execute
        JavaScript}, so anything a script loads afterwards (a tracking pixel,
        a lazy-loaded image, a carousel's later slides) is invisible to it.
        \smallskip
        \textbf{Which it could fix:} the first, by parsing the CSS for
        \texttt{url()} references and following them --- a dozen lines. The
        second it cannot fix, because fixing it means running a browser, which
        is a different kind of tool. Knowing which limitation is laziness and
        which is fundamental is the useful judgement here.
\end{ans}

\ansch{9 --- The Shopping Basket, and Where State Lives}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{What would change:} the two queries per request that the
        \texttt{db} engine spends reading and writing the session record. On
        this host that was worth about 20\% of the page --- 89\,ms to 71\,ms
        --- when those two queries were removed altogether by the
        \texttt{file} engine, and Redis would remove them from the database in
        the same way. \textbf{What would not change: the basket.} It is rows in
        \texttt{oc\_cart}, and the \texttt{Cart rows} column was 1 under both
        engines. \textbf{The number that settles it:} the session record is
        \textbf{18 bytes} and holds \texttt{\{"currency":"USD"\}}. Whatever you
        move it to, you are moving eighteen bytes. The right place to look for
        basket performance is the six cart queries an empty basket costs, not
        the store the session sits in.
  \item \textbf{Where they came from:} a session row is written on every
        request, before the visitor has asked for anything, so every page view
        by every crawler, uptime check and measurement harness created one. The
        645 rows here are the by-product of writing this handout.
        \textbf{Why they are not removed on departure:} nothing tells a server
        that a visitor has left --- HTTP has no ``goodbye''. Rows can therefore
        only expire on a clock, and \texttt{config\_session\_expire} is 86{,}400
        seconds. \textbf{What to measure first:} the proportion of sessions
        that ever acquire a basket or a login. If almost none do, the expiry is
        not the problem and robots are; shortening the expiry would then also
        shorten the life of the few baskets that matter, which is the opposite
        of what was wanted. Measure before tuning the number that is easiest
        to reach.
  \item \textbf{What each removes:} \texttt{down} removes the containers ---
        the running processes and their writable layers. The named volumes
        \texttt{dbdata} and \texttt{shopdata} are separate objects and are left
        alone, so the database files survive and the basket with them;
        \texttt{up -d} attaches the new containers to the same volumes and the
        shop resumes mid-visit. \texttt{down -v} additionally deletes those
        volumes, which is where every byte of durable state lives, so the
        entrypoint finds an empty database and reinstalls from scratch.
        \textbf{One other thing it destroys:} every product, category, order,
        customer and setting you have made since \chref{stack} --- in this
        measurement the shop came back in 17 seconds looking perfectly
        healthy, with all of it gone. Uploaded images go with the
        \texttt{shopdata} volume at the same time.
\end{ans}

\ansch{10 --- The Checkout and the Order State Machine}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Three things, in order.} (a)~\textbf{Drive the checkout
        end to end from a script and read every reply}, not the rendered
        pages. (b)~\textbf{Find the first step whose answer disagrees with the
        step before it} --- here, an address accepted with
        \texttt{success} and then reported missing. (c)~\textbf{Read the
        source for that one step}, which the previous two have now identified
        out of several thousand files. \textbf{What the measurement would have
        told you:} that the shipping quote answered
        \texttt{"Shipping address required!"} --- a fault, not a price
        objection. The shipping charges were never the question, and a shop
        that had lowered them would have changed nothing and concluded that
        customers were simply difficult.
  \item \textbf{Why the shares differ.} Time is spread across all eight
        steps because every one of them loads the framework, starts a session,
        rebuilds the basket and renders something --- fixed costs that have
        nothing to do with what the step is for. Writes are not spread: seven
        steps write almost nothing, because collecting an address changes a
        session, while \texttt{confirm} commits the whole order ---
        \texttt{oc\_order}, its products, its totals and its addresses --- in
        one burst. \textbf{What it implies at ten times the traffic:} optimise
        the \emph{fixed} cost, not \texttt{confirm}. Reads scale by caching
        and by the work of \chref{catalogue}; writes do not cache, but 55
        writes per order against 607 queries means the order itself is the
        small part. The 271 queries spent before the customer has committed to
        anything are the ones to attack.
  \item \textbf{Two reasons to refuse.} (a)~\textbf{They are evidence.}
        Each one is a customer who reached the payment button and stopped; the
        ratio of status-0 rows to real orders \emph{is} the abandonment rate,
        and deleting them destroys the only record of it the shop has.
        (b)~\textbf{A payment provider may still be looking for one.} A
        gateway's reply can arrive minutes or hours later, and it finds the
        order by an identifier that must still exist (\chref{gateway});
        deleting the row turns a late but successful payment into money
        received against nothing. \textbf{What to build instead:} a report
        that counts them by day and by last-reached step, and an archiving rule
        that moves rows older than the gateway's longest callback window --- so
        that nothing is destroyed and the live table stays small.
\end{ans}

\ansch{11 --- Shipping, Tax and Zones}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Where the 141.40 comes from.} It is not 20\% of the
        sub-total: $0.20 \times 702.00 = 140.40$, which is 1.00 short. The
        missing 1.00 is 20\% of the 5.00 shipping charge, so the base is
        $702.00 + 5.00 = 707.00$ and $0.20 \times 707.00 = 141.40$.
        \textbf{The two rule decisions this reveals.} (a)~\textbf{Shipping is
        taxable} in this configuration --- a jurisdictional choice, not
        arithmetic, and one that differs between countries. (b)~\textbf{The
        fixed eco tax is not itself taxed}: it contributed 6.00 to the total
        but nothing to the VAT base, so the two tax lines are applied to the
        same base in parallel rather than one on top of the other. Had they
        compounded, the VAT would have been $0.20 \times 713.00 = 142.60$.
  \item \textbf{What is wrong.} A base of \texttt{store} means every order is
        taxed as though it were delivered to the shop's own address in Lahore.
        \textbf{Which orders are affected:} only the UAE ones --- they are
        charged Pakistani sales tax on an export that should not carry it, and
        the shop is either over-collecting from the customer or absorbing the
        amount itself, while the correct treatment is recorded nowhere. Every
        domestic order is, by coincidence, correct. \textbf{Why nobody has
        noticed:} the configuration is right for the overwhelming majority of
        orders and for every order anyone has ever tested, because testing is
        done from Pakistan. A fault that is correct in the common case and
        wrong in the rare one does not announce itself. \textbf{What to
        change:} set the rule's base to \texttt{shipping}, and confine the
        rate's geo zone to Pakistan, so that a UAE delivery matches no rule
        and attracts no Pakistani tax. Then place an order to each, because a
        change made on reasoning alone has not been verified.
  \item \textbf{The failure mode in money.} Weight-based shipping charges from
        a band table keyed on total basket weight. A product with weight zero
        contributes nothing, so a basket of only zero-weight products falls in
        the lowest band --- usually free or near-free delivery --- and the shop
        pays the courier out of its own margin. With 600 of 4000 products
        affected, roughly one basket in seven will be understated, and the
        worst cases are the heaviest products whose weight was never entered.
        \textbf{Why no error appears:} zero is a valid weight. The band lookup
        succeeds, a quote is returned, the order completes, and every page
        returns 200 --- the same shape of silent failure as
        \chref{checkout}'s missing setting. \textbf{One change that makes it
        visible:} refuse to quote for a basket containing a zero-weight
        product, or, less disruptively, add a dashboard panel
        (\chref{dashboard}) counting enabled products with a weight of zero.
        The first prevents the loss; the second reports it. Either is better
        than an accurate method nobody maintains.
\end{ans}

\ansch{12 --- Discounts, Vouchers and Referrals}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{The setting} was the coupon total extension's \emph{sort
        order}: 4 as shipped, which places it before the tax line at 5, versus
        6, which places it after. \textbf{Why 14.04:} the discount is 70.20,
        and the VAT rate is 20\%. With the coupon first, the VAT base is
        $702.00 + 5.00 - 70.20 = 636.80$ and the VAT is 127.36; with it last,
        the base is $702.00 + 5.00 = 707.00$ and the VAT is 141.40. The
        difference $141.40 - 127.36 = 14.04$ is exactly $0.20 \times 70.20$ ---
        tax on the discount. \textbf{Which is correct: 770.16}, the shipped
        order. Tax is owed on \emph{consideration} --- what the customer
        actually pays --- and the customer was never asked for the 70.20, so no
        tax is due on it. Charging 784.20 collects 14.04 of tax on money that
        changed hands with nobody.
  \item \textbf{The break-even uplift.} With margin $m = 0.25$ and discount
        $d = 0.15$, the multiple is $m/(m-d) = 0.25/0.10 = \mathbf{2.5}$ ---
        the shop needs \textbf{150\% more orders}, two and a half times the
        volume, to make the same profit. \textbf{Two reasons the real
        requirement is higher.} (a)~\textbf{Cannibalisation}: a permanent code
        for \emph{loyal} customers goes, by definition, to people who already
        buy. Every one of them who would have bought anyway is pure loss, and
        no new volume is created to offset it. (b)~\textbf{Variable costs scale
        with volume}: 150\% more orders means 150\% more picking, packing,
        delivery and support, so the gross-margin arithmetic above understates
        the cost of getting there --- and a permanent code also resets the
        reference price, so the 15\% is not recoverable later.
        \textbf{A cheaper alternative:} store credit issued \emph{after} an
        order --- say 5\% back, usable on the next purchase. It costs less (not
        every credit is redeemed), it cannot be found by someone searching for
        a code, it rewards repeat buying rather than discounting a single sale,
        and it brings the customer back rather than merely letting them leave
        cheaper.
  \item \textbf{What to tell the client.} That the platform has no gift
        vouchers at all --- no tables, no totals extension, only a leftover
        demonstration image from the version that did --- and that the outline
        naming them reflects a 2010 book rather than current software. Say it
        before quoting, not after. \textbf{What the platform already provides:}
        the \emph{spending} half. Store credit
        (\texttt{oc\_customer\_transaction} and the \texttt{credit} total) is a
        balance on an account that draws down across orders, handles partial
        payment, and already sits in the totals pipeline.
        \textbf{What must be built:} the \emph{buying} half --- selling a
        voucher as a purchasable product; generating a code on order
        completion; delivering it to a named recipient; and redeeming it into
        \emph{a different customer's} credit, with the balance, expiry and
        single-use guarantees that implies. That is a module
        (\chref{modules}), and the hard parts are the two ways it can be spent
        twice: two simultaneous redemptions of the same code, and a redemption
        on an order that is later refunded.
\end{ans}

\ansch{13 --- Web Payment Systems}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Two ways to ship goods you are never paid for.}
        (a)~\textbf{Authorisation mistaken for capture.} The gateway reserved
        the funds and answered ``success'', but nothing was ever claimed. The
        reservation lapses after a few days and the money returns to the
        customer, who has the goods. (b)~\textbf{Capture followed by a
        chargeback.} The money arrived and was later taken back by the issuer
        --- on a disputed or fraudulent card, this is the shop's loss, not the
        acquirer's. \textbf{What to check in the documentation:} for the first,
        whether the endpoint you call performs authorisation only or
        authorisation-and-capture, and if the former, which call captures and
        when it must happen. For the second, the provider's chargeback terms:
        how long the window is, what evidence wins a dispute, and whether a
        delivery signature is among it. Both questions have documented answers,
        and neither is visible in the integration's happy path.
  \item \textbf{Net margin at a 10\% refusal rate.} Per 100 orders: 90
        delivered at $900 - 60 - 250 = $~Rs~590 each, so $90 \times 590 =
        $~Rs~53{,}100; 10 refused at $-$Rs~500 each, so $-$Rs~5{,}000.
        \textbf{Net Rs~48{,}100}, against Rs~37{,}200 at a 20\% refusal rate
        --- an improvement of Rs~10{,}900, or \textbf{29\%}.
        \textbf{Against removing the courier fee entirely:} the fee is Rs~60 on
        each of 80 delivered orders, $80 \times 60 = $~Rs~4{,}800, which raises
        the net to Rs~42{,}000 --- an improvement of \textbf{13\%}. So halving
        the refusal rate is worth more than twice as much as winning a free COD
        service, and the courier would never agree to the second while the
        first is entirely within the shop's own control. \textbf{The lesson:}
        negotiating hard over the thing you cannot change while ignoring the
        thing you can is exactly backwards, and the arithmetic says so in one
        line.
  \item \textbf{Four things, in order.} (1)~\textbf{Can you legally receive the
        money?} Foreign-currency receipts by a Pakistani merchant are subject
        to State Bank rules, and the answer determines whether the project is
        possible at all. (2)~\textbf{Will an acquirer accept this business?}
        Cross-border card acceptance is underwritten against the risk that the
        shop does not deliver, and a new shop with no trading history is often
        declined. (3)~\textbf{What does it cost, including the dispute rate?}
        International card-not-present carries both a higher discount rate and
        a materially higher chargeback rate than domestic. (4)~\textbf{What
        will you ship, and how will you prove you shipped it?} Winning a
        dispute requires delivery evidence, and international delivery often
        produces none. \textbf{Most likely to stop the project: the second.}
        The technical integration is a week; acquirer onboarding for
        cross-border acceptance by a small Pakistani merchant is the step that
        fails --- and it fails after everything has been built, if you leave it
        until last. That is why it belongs second on this list and not fourth.
\end{ans}

\ansch{14 --- Taking Payment for Orders}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{What a 1-second timeout actually costs.} From the measured
        table it cuts off \textbf{23 of 196 answered requests, 11.7\%} --- not
        a few stragglers but one payment in eight and a half, each with an
        \emph{unknown} outcome rather than a failed one, so the customer may
        already have been charged. The colleague's reasoning is sound for a
        bell-shaped distribution, and this one is not bell-shaped: the median
        is 321\,ms and the 95th percentile is 3{,}774\,ms, so ``three times the
        median'' lands in the empty gap between the two groups.
        \textbf{What to propose: 5 seconds}, which cut off nothing at all, and
        the measurement to justify it. The customer waits longer in the rare
        slow case, and the shop stops discarding good payments. If five seconds
        is judged too long to make anyone wait, the answer is not a shorter
        timeout but an asynchronous design --- accept, show a pending page, and
        resolve from the provider's callback.
  \item \textbf{Why the middle is flat.} Because the latency distribution is
        \emph{bimodal}: one group answers in under about half a second and a
        second group takes between two and five seconds, with very little in
        between. A timeout placed in the gap therefore catches almost nothing
        extra --- going from 1\,s to 3\,s rescued two requests in two hundred.
        \textbf{What the shape tells you that an average could not:} that there
        are two distinct code paths inside the provider rather than one
        variable one --- a fast path, and something slower behind it such as a
        risk check or an internal retry --- and that the only meaningful
        choices are ``before the slow path'' and ``after it''. An average of
        700\,ms invites tuning around 700\,ms, which is precisely the region
        where no choice makes any difference at all.
  \item \textbf{Without a key.} The second request is a new, valid
        authorisation. The provider has no way to tell it from a customer
        legitimately buying twice, so it charges again and returns a different
        transaction identifier --- in the measurement
        \texttt{f59594f7d23c4446} and \texttt{8171b6e85f9b4766}, both
        approved. The customer has paid twice and the shop has one order.
        \textbf{With a key.} The provider recognises it, returns the first
        answer unchanged --- same transaction identifier, in 5\,ms --- and
        charges nothing. \textbf{Where it is stored: on the order row},
        generated once when the order is created. \textbf{Why generating it
        when the button is pressed would not work:} the retry is a new request
        and would generate a new key, which is a key the provider has never
        seen, so it charges again. The key must identify \emph{the payment
        attempt for this order}, not the HTTP request --- and the order is the
        only thing that survives between the two.
\end{ans}

\ansch{15 --- User Account Management}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Is cost 12 affordable?} Assumptions, stated: 400 orders a day;
        perhaps three logins per order once failed attempts, repeat visits and
        account pages are counted, so about 1{,}200 logins a day; and traffic
        is not flat, so assume the busiest hour carries 20\% of the day's
        logins --- 240 in an hour, about \textbf{0.07 logins per second} on
        average and perhaps one per second in a burst. Cost 12 delivers
        \textbf{5 logins per second} on one core. The headroom is more than
        fiftyfold, so \textbf{yes, comfortably} --- and cost 13, at 2 per
        second, would still be adequate. \textbf{What else must change at the
        same time:} the timing leak. Cost 12 raises the enumeration gap from
        about 65\,ms to about 220\,ms, four times louder, so the dummy-hash fix
        must ship \emph{with} the cost change rather than after it. Existing
        hashes also remain at cost 10 until each customer next logs in, so the
        code must rehash on successful login or the change never reaches
        anybody.
  \item \textbf{The cause.} A wrong password on a registered address must be
        checked against the stored hash, which costs one bcrypt verify ---
        measured at 60.3\,ms at cost 10. An unknown address matches no row, so
        there is nothing to verify and the request returns early. The 65.5\,ms
        gap is that verify. \textbf{What an attacker does with it:} runs a list
        of email addresses through the login form with any password at all, and
        keeps the slow ones. That is a confirmed customer list --- saleable in
        itself, and it makes credential stuffing and phishing against this
        shop's customers far cheaper, because every address is known to be
        live. \textbf{The one-line fix:} when no customer matches, verify the
        supplied password against a fixed dummy hash and discard the result.
        \textbf{Why it feels wrong to write:} it is deliberately wasted work in
        the hot path of a login form, and every instinct a programmer has says
        to delete it. A reviewer who does not know why it is there will delete
        it, which is exactly why the reason belongs in a comment beside it.
  \item \textbf{The risk you are accepting} by removing lockout: unlimited
        online password guessing against any account. At 60\,ms per attempt a
        single attacker can try tens of thousands of passwords an hour against
        one account, which breaks any password a customer actually chooses.
        \textbf{The risk you are removing:} anyone who knows a customer's email
        address can lock that customer out at will --- five wrong guesses, an
        hour at a time, repeatedly --- so the complaint is real, and the
        customers complaining may be victims rather than the forgetful.
        \textbf{A design that addresses both better than either:} replace the
        hard count with \emph{progressive delay} keyed on the address being
        guessed --- no delay for the first three attempts, then a delay that
        doubles with each further failure and resets on success. A customer who
        mistypes twice notices nothing; an attacker is throttled to a few
        guesses an hour, and no account is ever locked, so there is nothing to
        abuse. Add site-wide rate limiting by source address to catch the
        attacker who spreads attempts across many accounts --- which neither
        lockout nor per-account delay sees at all --- and offer a second factor
        to the customers who want one.
\end{ans}

\ansch{16 --- Securing the Shop}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Two independent reasons.} (a)~\textbf{Apache never reads the
        file.} Debian's \texttt{apache2.conf} sets \texttt{AllowOverride None}
        for the document root, so \texttt{.htaccess} files are ignored
        entirely --- the rename is correct and has no effect. (b)~\textbf{Even
        read, it would not be the whole fix.} The shipped file blocks
        \texttt{system/storage} by rewrite and sets one header; it says nothing
        about the content security policy, the referrer policy, the permissions
        policy, the version banners or the wildcard CORS headers, which were
        eight of the nine findings. \textbf{One command to demonstrate it:}
        write a marker into the error log and fetch it ---
        \texttt{docker compose exec shop sh -c 'echo MARKER >>
        system/storage/logs/error.log'} then \texttt{curl -s
        http://localhost:8090/system/storage/logs/error.log}. If the marker
        comes back, the control is not working whatever the file is called.
        \textbf{The general lesson:} a control that fails open and fails
        silently is indistinguishable from a control that works, until
        somebody measures the outcome. Verify the result, never the action ---
        and the gap between ``I installed it'' and ``it does something'' is
        where almost every real breach lives.
  \item \textbf{Why none of them touched the shop's code.} Because none of them
        was a programming fault. Every one was a property of how the software
        was \emph{served}: which headers Apache attaches, what it will serve
        from which directory, and what it volunteers about itself. The
        application had already escaped its SQL, auto-escaped its output,
        tokenised its forms and hashed its passwords properly. \textbf{What
        that says about inheriting a shop:} look at the deployment first. The
        framework's defences are written once by people who do this full time
        and are the same in every installation; the deployment is different in
        every installation and is usually nobody's job. \textbf{Which remaining
        finding first: move \texttt{system/storage/} out of the web root.} The
        admin path is the one still failing the battery, but renaming it only
        defeats automated scanners, while the storage directory holds the error
        log, the sessions and every upload --- and today it is protected only
        by a block in a configuration file that the next administrator may
        edit, replace or lose during an upgrade. Moving it removes the risk
        instead of covering it, and is the control that survives being
        forgotten.
  \item \textbf{Two mechanisms, any two of the three.}
        (a)~\textbf{\texttt{Header always unset} on a successful response.}
        \texttt{mod\_headers} keeps two tables --- \texttt{always} for every
        response including errors, and the default for successful ones only.
        A header PHP sets on a 200 lives in the second, so the directive whose
        name sounds more thorough touches nothing.
        (b)~\textbf{\texttt{ServerTokens} after a reload.} It is consumed when
        Apache starts and used to build the banner once; a graceful reload
        applies every neighbouring directive and skips this one.
        (c)~\textbf{Filename ordering in \texttt{conf-enabled}.} Apache reads
        the directory in filename order and the last setting of a server-level
        directive wins, so \texttt{security-ecad.conf} lost to
        \texttt{security.conf} because \texttt{-} sorts before \texttt{.}.
        \textbf{The habit that catches all three without knowing any of them:}
        \emph{measure the observable outcome before and after every change, from
        outside the system}. Not ``is the directive present'', not ``did Apache
        accept the config'' --- all three of these produced a configuration
        Apache accepted without a single warning --- but ``does the response
        now contain the header, and does the URL now refuse to serve the
        file''. One script, run twice, found all three in the time it would
        have taken to re-read the file once. That is the entire argument for
        the measured strand in this course, in its most concrete form.
\end{ans}

\ansch{19 --- Extending the Platform: Your First Module}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{The answer.} Five instances cost 20\,ms on a 76\,ms page ---
        about 4\,ms and exactly 4 queries each --- and of those four queries
        \emph{one} is the module's own. The other three are the framework
        finding the instance's settings, its translations and its template
        override. So the measured cost of ``being a module'' is 3 queries, and
        the cost of the feature is 1. \textbf{Editing the theme instead} would
        remove those three and keep the one, saving roughly 3\,ms per instance
        --- at the price of a change the next upgrade overwrites, that
        \texttt{git status} will not distinguish from the vendor's code, and
        that cannot be placed twice or disabled without another edit.
        \textbf{What to measure before agreeing to anything:} whether the page
        is actually too slow, and against what target. A 96\,ms page with five
        modules is not a performance problem --- \chref{ux} measured 600\,KB of
        assets after it and \chref{deploy} measured the saturation point at
        five concurrent clients. Optimising 3\,ms while 600\,KB goes unexamined
        is the error this whole course is written against.
  \item \textbf{When it stops working.} The moment the module is placed a
        \emph{second} time with a different threshold. Both instances will then
        read the same global value, so both show the same products, and
        whichever instance was configured last silently wins --- or, if no such
        global setting exists at all, both read null and fall back to the
        default, so neither reflects what was typed. \textbf{What the
        shopkeeper will report:} ``I set the second panel to 20 and it still
        shows the same four items as the first one.'' They will not report a
        bug in the module; they will report that the admin panel does not save.
        That is the characteristic shape of this fault --- the symptom points
        at the form, and the cause is in the render method, which is the one
        place nobody looks because it is visibly working.
  \item \textbf{How to decide.} First ask whether a \emph{setting} reaches it:
        a custom field on the customer or the address, or a product attribute,
        covers more requests of this kind than people expect, and a setting
        survives every upgrade. If not, ask whether the platform offers an
        \emph{extension point} at the place you need --- for checkout there are
        events on the controllers, and an event handler is an extension. Only
        if neither reaches do you consider editing, and then the question is
        whether the field is worth owning a merge conflict for ever.
        \textbf{What I would need to know first:} who fills the field in and
        who reads it; whether it must appear on the \emph{order} as well as in
        the session, because an order copies its data at checkout
        (\chref{checkout}) and a field that is not copied disappears from
        history; and whether it is required, because a required field changes
        the validation path and therefore the completion rate.
        \textbf{What I would write down whichever I chose:} the decision, the
        two options rejected and why, and --- if it was an edit --- the file,
        the version it was made against, and what must be re-checked at the
        next upgrade. The note is the deliverable; the code is the easy part.
\end{ans}

\ansch{20 --- Deployment, Maintenance and Performance}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{The mechanism.} At five concurrent clients the server's
        processors are fully occupied and nothing is waiting. Past that point
        the extra requests do not find spare capacity --- they join a queue. A
        queued request still consumes memory, a connection and a worker slot,
        and the context switching between twenty workers costs real time, so
        the server does \emph{less} useful work while every request waits
        longer. That is why throughput fell from 42.1 to 38.7 while the median
        rose from 95\,ms to 455\,ms. \textbf{What the owner should do before a
        sale day:} know the number, and decide what happens past it --- queue
        politely with a wait page, or shed load --- rather than discovering it
        at the busiest hour of the year. \textbf{One change that moves the
        turning point:} cache whole pages for visitors who are not logged in.
        It removes PHP and the database from the common path entirely, which is
        where every millisecond in the measurement was spent; the saturation
        point then becomes a property of the web server rather than of the
        application, and that is a different order of magnitude.
  \item \textbf{Both ends.} \textbf{At one client} the processor is mostly
        idle: a request arrives, is compiled and served, and then nothing
        happens until the next one. Removing compilation work from a processor
        that has spare capacity buys little, so 1.3$\times$. \textbf{At twenty}
        the bottleneck has moved from the processor to the \emph{queue}: every
        request waits behind nineteen others, and shortening each one by a
        third shortens the queue proportionally but does not change the fact
        that there is one, so the benefit is diluted to 1.4$\times$. \textbf{In
        the middle}, at the saturation point, the processor is the binding
        constraint and nothing else is --- so work removed from it converts
        almost one-for-one into throughput, hence 2.0$\times$. \textbf{What it
        implies about when to benchmark:} measure at or near the saturation
        point, because that is where an optimisation's effect is both largest
        and most honestly represented. Benchmarking a single client flatters
        nothing and shows nothing; benchmarking far past saturation measures
        the queue and attributes it to your change.
  \item \textbf{The case, in terms a client will act on.} Not ``your backups
        might be corrupt'' --- that is a hypothetical and clients discount
        hypotheticals. Instead: ``We do not know how long the shop would be
        closed. I can find out this afternoon.'' Recovery time is a business
        number, it belongs in the same sentence as a day's takings, and nobody
        has ever been given it. \textbf{What I would do:} bring up a second
        empty stack, restore last night's dump into it, open the shop, and
        check that the catalogue, the orders and the settings are all there.
        \textbf{How long:} under an hour for this shop, including writing it
        down; the restore itself was 6.4 seconds on the demonstration
        catalogue and will be minutes on a real one. \textbf{What I would
        measure and record:} the restore time, the age of the newest data in
        the dump --- which is how much the shop would lose, and is a different
        number from how long it is down --- and anything that was \emph{not} in
        the backup, which in this stack is the uploaded images in the
        \texttt{shopdata} volume. That last item is the one such a test
        usually finds, and finding it is the entire point.
\end{ans}

\ansch{21 --- SEO and Digital Marketing}
\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{What has happened.} The client switched on
        \texttt{config\_seo\_url}, which is the \emph{application} half of
        readable URLs: the shop now looks every link up in
        \texttt{oc\_seo\_url} and writes \texttt{/en-gb/catalog/desktops} into

        back into \texttt{index.php?\_route\_=en-gb/catalog/desktops} before
        PHP sees it --- is absent, because it ships in \texttt{.htaccess} and
        \chref{security} measured \texttt{AllowOverride None}, which means
        Apache never reads \texttt{.htaccess} on this stack. \textbf{Which half
        is absent: the server half.} \textbf{The command that demonstrates it:}
        \texttt{curl -s -o /dev/null -w "\%\{http\_code\}"
        http://the-shop/en-gb/catalog/desktops} returns 404, while the same
        page via \texttt{index.php?\_route\_=...} returns 200 --- which proves
        the application is right and the server was never told.
        \textbf{Why nothing appeared in any log:} a 404 is an ordinary
        response. It is not an error, not an exception, and not a failure of
        anything; the server was asked for a path it has no rule for and said
        so, correctly. The shop is working exactly as configured, and the
        configuration is wrong.
  \item \textbf{Which is luck and which is design.} The distinct titles are
        \textbf{luck}: the title is the product's name, and the demonstration
        products happen to have different names. A shop selling the same
        garment in four sizes as four products would have four identical
        titles and the same code would produce them. The missing descriptions
        are \textbf{design} --- or rather, the absence of design: the field
        exists in \texttt{oc\_product\_description}, the demonstration data
        leaves it empty, and the platform writes nothing in its place.
        \textbf{What happens on those pages:} the search engine composes its
        own summary from whatever text it finds, which on a product page is
        usually the navigation menu --- so the snippet a customer reads in the
        results is a list of categories rather than a description of the
        product. \textbf{For 50{,}000 products:} do not write them by hand.
        Generate a reasonable default from data you already have --- name,
        manufacturer, category and price --- as a module
        (\chref{modules}), which gets every page off the floor; then write
        proper descriptions by hand for the few hundred products that actually
        sell, because those are the only ones where the difference between
        adequate and good is worth an afternoon.
  \item \textbf{What each absence costs.} \textbf{No sitemap} means a crawler
        finds pages only by following links from the home page, so anything
        several hops away is effectively invisible --- and depth grows with
        catalogue size, since a 50{,}000-product shop paginates its categories
        into hundreds of pages that \texttt{robots.txt} itself blocks.
        \textbf{No canonical links} means the same product reached through two
        categories, or with a sort parameter, is several URLs with identical
        content competing with one another, and the engine picks one
        arbitrarily. \textbf{For 19 products the canonical matters more:} every
        page is one click from the home page so nothing is unfindable, but
        nineteen products across several categories already generate duplicate
        paths. \textbf{For 50{,}000 the sitemap matters more:} duplication is
        still a problem, but it is a problem about pages that are at least
        known to exist, and the greater loss by far is the tens of thousands
        that are never reached at all. \textbf{The difference:} canonical links
        are about which of the pages a crawler found should count; a sitemap is
        about whether it found them. Being counted wrongly is a smaller harm
        than not being counted.
\end{ans}

  \item \textbf{The ranking: the 6 orders are higher risk than the 645
        sessions}, by a wide margin, and the volume points the other way.
        \textbf{Justification:} a session row holds an address, a timestamp
        and a currency for an unidentified visitor who was probably a crawler
        (\chref{basket}); leaked, it is noise. An order row holds a named
        person's home address, telephone number, email address and a list of
        what they bought, delivered to where they live; leaked, six of them
        would do more harm than all 645 sessions together. Risk is harm per
        record times records, and the first term dominates by orders of
        magnitude. \textbf{Retention. Sessions: 24 hours}, which is already the
        configured expiry --- they exist to make the next click work and have
        no purpose after the visit, so the only argument for keeping them is
        that nothing deletes them, which is not an argument.
        \textbf{Orders: years}, because they are accounting records with a
        statutory life and are the contract (\chref{legal}'s opening). The
        \emph{personal} parts of an old order may be reducible --- a telephone
        number is not needed to satisfy a tax inspector --- but that is a
        decision to take advice on, not one to take alone.
        \textbf{Which I would be more reluctant to delete: the orders}, and the
        reason is the opposite of the risk ranking. They are the most dangerous
        thing the shop holds \emph{and} the thing it is least free to discard,
        which is exactly why they deserve the most protection rather than the
        most deletion. The uncomfortable pairing of those two facts is the
        whole of data governance in one example.
  \item \textbf{The reply.}
        \smallskip
        ``No, and I want to be clear about why rather than just refusing.
        Storing card numbers moves us from the shortest PCI DSS questionnaire
        to the longest --- from a handful of questions about keeping our site
        out of the way, to several hundred requirements and an external audit,
        on us, annually. That is a real recurring cost and a real liability,
        and it arrives the moment the first number is stored, not when
        something goes wrong.
        \smallskip
        What our competitor is doing is almost certainly not what it looks
        like. The convenience you are describing is \emph{tokenisation}: the
        provider stores the card and gives us a meaningless reference, so the
        customer does not retype anything and we never hold a number. Every
        provider we would use offers it, it is usually a configuration change
        rather than development, and it keeps the card where it is already
        somebody else's full-time job to protect.
        \smallskip
        I can have that working this week.''
        \smallskip
        \textbf{If the client insisted:} say once more, in writing, what the
        consequence is and who carries it --- the merchant, not the developer
        and not the provider. Then decline to implement it. This is not a
        matter of taste: it is a documented obligation the client would be
        taking on unknowingly, and building it after explaining that would make
        me a party to it rather than a contractor on it.
\end{ans}

\begin{ans}
  \item \textbf{Five likely causes, in order.} (1)~\textbf{A port already in
        use} on their machine --- 8090 or 8091 is the commonest, and this
        handout hit it in \chref{stack}. (2)~\textbf{A step that lives only in
        your shell history}: a setting you changed once in the admin panel, a
        SQL statement you ran by hand, a file you copied. It works for you
        because you did it and forgot. (3)~\textbf{Data that is not in the
        repository} --- product images, a database dump --- because the volume
        held it and the volume is not the repository. (4)~\textbf{A line ending
        or path assumption}: an entrypoint script saved with CRLF will not run
        in the container, and the error is unhelpful. (5)~\textbf{A different
        Docker version or no Docker at all.} \textbf{How to find out without
        their machine:} ask for three things --- the exact command they ran,
        the complete output, and \texttt{docker compose ps}. Those three
        separate ``it never started'' from ``it started and the shop is not
        installed'' from ``it is running and they are at the wrong address'',
        which is the whole diagnostic tree. \textbf{What should have been done
        in week 12:} build it from scratch on a machine that is not yours ---
        \texttt{docker compose down -v} at minimum, a classmate's laptop
        ideally --- because every one of the five causes is invisible on the
        machine where the shop was built and obvious on any other.
  \item \textbf{What goes in the report:} both numbers, the index definition,
        the catalogue size, the machine, and the command that produces them ---
        and then the honest sentence: the index made the page 8\% slower, and
        here is what we think is happening. \textbf{What to investigate
        first:} whether the query is using the index at all. \texttt{EXPLAIN}
        before and after is one command and splits the result in two: if the
        optimiser ignored it, the question is why (a function on the column, as
        \chref{catalogue} found with \texttt{LCASE}, or a selectivity it did
        not like); if it used it and was slower, the index is being read in a
        different order from the table and the scattered reads cost more than
        the sort it saved --- which is exactly what \chref{catalogue} measured
        at 50{,}000 products, twice. \textbf{Why it is worth more marks:} 15\,ms
        is within noise for a single run, so the first thing it tests is
        whether you measured properly --- repeats, a median, a warm cache. And
        if it survives that, it is a real result that contradicts the
        textbook rule, which is the kind of thing this course exists to teach
        you to notice. A result that went as expected demonstrates that you
        can follow instructions; this one demonstrates that you can measure.
  \item \textbf{Three things still wrong with the shop as this handout left
        it.} (1)~\textbf{The admin panel is still at \texttt{/admin/}}
