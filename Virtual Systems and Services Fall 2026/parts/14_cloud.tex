\section{What Makes a Cloud a Cloud}

``Cloud'' is used loosely enough to be useless as a technical term, so it is
worth having a test. The US National Institute of Standards and Technology
published one in 2011 and it has aged well: five characteristics, all five
required.

\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{3.7cm}L{5.6cm}L{5.2cm}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
\textbf{Characteristic} & \textbf{What it requires} & \textbf{What fails the
test} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\term{On-demand self-service} & You provision capacity yourself, without
talking to anybody & A hosting provider where a new server is a support ticket
and two days \\
\term{Broad network access} & Reachable over standard protocols from standard
clients & A service requiring a leased line and a vendor client \\
\term{Resource pooling} & Many tenants share one pool; you do not know or care
which hardware & A dedicated rack assigned to you permanently \\
\term{Rapid elasticity} & Capacity grows and \emph{shrinks} quickly, apparently
without limit & Scaling up in minutes but requiring a contract change to scale
down \\
\term{Measured service} & Usage is metered and billed by what you used &
A flat annual fee regardless of consumption \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\begin{conceptbox}[The Clause That Catches Most ``Private Clouds'']
Four of the five are easy to satisfy with a well-run vSphere estate. The one
that is not is \emph{rapid elasticity}, and specifically the word
\emph{shrinks}.

\smallskip
A department that buys three hosts can provision guests on demand, pool them,
meter them and reach them over the network. What it cannot do is return
capacity. The hardware is bought; shutting a guest down saves a little
electricity and nothing else. Elasticity in the NIST sense requires that unused
capacity stops costing money, and that requires somebody else to be able to
rent it.

\smallskip
This is the honest difference between a private cloud and a well-managed
virtualization estate, and it is worth stating plainly because a great deal of
marketing depends on blurring it. What a private cloud genuinely buys is the
\emph{interface} --- self-service, API-driven, metered --- which is valuable on
its own. It does not buy the economics.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{conceptbox}[``Serverless'' Means Somebody Else's Server, Started Fast]
FaaS deserves one clarification because the name actively misleads. There are
servers. What is removed is any notion of a server you provision, size or keep
running: you supply a function, the provider runs it when something calls it,
and you pay per invocation and per millisecond.

\smallskip
\chref{lightweight} is how it is implemented. A function invocation on AWS
Lambda runs in a Firecracker microVM, which is why the model is economically
possible at all: an isolation boundary that costs 125 ms and 5 MB can be
created per request, and one that costs 25 s and 400 MB cannot.

\smallskip
So the commercial model in this chapter rests directly on the engineering of
the last. That is the clearest instance in this book of a technical property
--- boot time --- determining what can be sold.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Deployment Models, and What an Instance Is}

\term{Public} is a provider's shared pool. \term{Private} is a pool for one
organisation, on its premises or hosted. \term{Hybrid} connects the two, with
workloads placed by policy. \term{Multi-cloud} spreads across more than one
provider --- usually for leverage or regulatory reasons, occasionally for
availability, and always at the cost of using the least-common-denominator of
both.

A provider's capacity is organised as \term{regions} (a geographic area, with
its own pricing and its own legal jurisdiction) containing
\term{availability zones} (independent data centres, separate power and
cooling, linked by low-latency fibre). The rule that follows: spread across
zones for availability, across regions for disaster recovery and for data
residency.

\begin{alertbox}[An instance type is a promise about scheduling, not just a size]
Instance families are the vocabulary of \chref{cpumem} made commercial.
General purpose, compute optimised, memory optimised and storage optimised
differ in their core-to-memory ratio --- and, less visibly, in whether their
vCPUs are oversubscribed.

\smallskip
A \emph{burstable} instance (AWS's \texttt{t} family, and its equivalents) is
explicitly oversubscribed: you are granted a baseline fraction of a core and
accumulate credits when below it. Exhaust the credits and you are throttled to
the baseline, which may be 5\% of a core. The ready time of \chref{cpumem} is
here sold as a product feature, and a great many ``the server suddenly became
slow after three weeks'' incidents are exhausted CPU credits.

\smallskip
Read the oversubscription policy of any instance family before benchmarking on
it, and never benchmark on a burstable one.
\end{alertbox}

\section{Paying For It}

Three ways to buy the same instance, differing in what you commit to.

\rowcolors{2}{white}{lightbg}
\begin{longtable}{@{}L{2.8cm}L{3.4cm}L{3.0cm}L{5.3cm}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
\textbf{Model} & \textbf{You commit to} & \textbf{Typical saving} &
\textbf{Suitable for} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\term{On-demand} & Nothing & --- & Unpredictable load, development, anything
short-lived \\
\term{Reserved} / savings plan & One or three years of spend & 34\% for one
year, \textbf{55\% for three} (no upfront, EC2 instance plan) & Steady baseline
load you are confident will still exist \\
\term{Spot} & Nothing, but can be reclaimed on short notice & 70--90\% &
Batch, CI, rendering, anything that can checkpoint and restart \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\begin{worked}[Three Years, On Premises and in the Cloud]
The forty workloads of \chref{what}, each needing 4 vCPUs and 16 GB. Compare
three years on premises with three years on AWS. Figures are US list prices for
\texttt{us-east-1}, checked in October 2026; \textbf{re-check before quoting
them}, because they move.

\smallskip
\textbf{On premises.} From \chref{what}: three hosts, 13.3:1 consolidation.
\begin{tight}
  \item Hardware: $3 \times \$18{,}000 = \$54{,}000$
  \item Shared storage array: $\$20{,}000$
  \item Power and cooling: 2.1 kW at PUE 1.6 is 3.36 kW;
        $3.36 \times 8{,}760 \times 3 = 88{,}300$ kWh at \$0.12 $=
        \$10{,}600$
  \item Rack space and facility: \$500 a month $= \$18{,}000$
  \item Staff: 0.3 of an engineer at \$45{,}000 $= \$40{,}500$
\end{tight}
\[ \text{total} = 54{,}000 + 20{,}000 + 10{,}600 + 18{,}000 + 40{,}500
   = \mathbf{\$143{,}100} \]

\smallskip
\textbf{Cloud, on demand.} An \texttt{m7i.xlarge} is 4 vCPU and 16 GB at
\$0.2016 per hour:
\[ 0.2016 \times 8{,}760 = \$1{,}766 \text{ per instance-year} \]
\[ 1{,}766 \times 3 \times 40 = \mathbf{\$211{,}900} \]
Plus 100 GB of gp3 storage each at \$0.08 per GB-month
($40 \times 100 \times 0.08 \times 36 = \$11{,}520$) and 2 TB a month of egress
at \$0.09 per GB ($\$6{,}480$ over three years):
\[ 211{,}900 + 11{,}520 + 6{,}480 = \mathbf{\$229{,}900} \]

\smallskip
\textbf{Cloud, three-year reservation.} A three-year EC2 instance savings plan
with no upfront payment saves 55\%:
\[ 211{,}900 \times 0.45 = \$95{,}400 \]
\[ 95{,}400 + 11{,}520 + 6{,}480 = \mathbf{\$113{,}400} \]

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- the comparison.}
\begin{tight}
  \item On premises: \$143{,}100
  \item Cloud, on demand: \$229{,}900 --- \textbf{61\% more}
  \item Cloud, three-year reserved: \$113{,}400 --- \textbf{21\% less}
\end{tight}

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 2 --- so which is it?} Both of the usual slogans are wrong. The
same workload on the same provider is either substantially cheaper or
substantially more expensive than owning the hardware, and \emph{the only thing
that changed was the purchasing decision}. A team that lifts and shifts onto
on-demand instances and never revisits it will conclude the cloud is expensive,
and will be right about their own bill and wrong about the cloud.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 3 --- what the arithmetic leaves out, in both directions.}

\emph{Favouring the cloud:} the on-premises figure assumes the three hosts are
right. Buy for peak and they are idle; buy for average and you have an outage
at peak. It also ignores the refresh in year four, the spare host you should
have, and the fact that 0.3 of an engineer is not a thing you can hire.

\emph{Favouring on premises:} the cloud figure assumes forty instances running
continuously for three years, which is the case least suited to the cloud. If
half the workloads could run only in office hours --- $8 \times 5$ instead of
$24 \times 7$, a 76\% reduction for those --- the cloud figure falls by roughly
\$36{,}000 more, and nothing equivalent is available on premises.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 4 --- the honest conclusion.} The decision is not technical. It
turns on how variable the load is, how confident you are of a three-year
commitment, and whether you have the staff. Steady and predictable favours
owning; variable or uncertain favours renting; and whichever you choose,
revisiting the purchasing model annually is worth more than the choice itself.
\end{worked}

\begin{alertbox}[Egress is the charge that surprises people]
Compute, storage and inbound traffic are priced in ways that feel proportionate.
Outbound data --- \term{egress} --- is not. At around \$0.09 per GB, moving
100 TB out of a cloud costs roughly \$9{,}000, and moving it \emph{in} is free.

\smallskip
That asymmetry is the economic shape of \term{lock-in}: nothing prevents you
leaving, and the bill for leaving grows with how much you have stored. Price
the exit when you design the architecture, not when you need it. Keep the data
you may have to move somewhere its egress is cheap, and know the figure.
\end{alertbox}

\section{Shared Responsibility}

Every provider publishes a version of the same diagram, and every provider's
version says the same thing: they are responsible for the security \emph{of}
the cloud, and you are responsible for security \emph{in} it.

Concretely, under IaaS the provider owns the physical facility, the hardware,
the hypervisor and the network fabric. You own the guest operating system and
its patching, your application, your data and its encryption, your identity and
access configuration, and your firewall rules.

\begin{conceptbox}[Which Side Is This Failure On?]
Nearly every large cloud data breach of the past decade has fallen on the
customer's side of that line, and usually on the same two items.

\begin{tight}
  \item An object store left publicly readable. The provider's default is
        private; somebody changed it.
  \item Credentials committed to a public repository, or an over-broad role
        attached to an instance.
\end{tight}

\smallskip
Neither is a cloud vulnerability. Both are configuration, and both are on your
side of the diagram. Meanwhile the provider's side --- the hypervisor of
\chref{platforms}, the isolation of \chref{security} --- has a strong record,
which is the opposite of what most risk discussions assume.

\smallskip
The practical reading: when evaluating cloud risk, spend your effort on your
own configuration, your identity model and your data, because that is where the
incidents actually are.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Comparing on-demand cloud prices with owned hardware.} It is
        the most expensive way to buy and the least representative. Compare
        like with like: a three-year commitment against a three-year asset.
  \item \textbf{Calling a vSphere estate a private cloud.} Check it against the
        five characteristics, especially whether capacity can be \emph{returned}.
  \item \textbf{Lifting and shifting without resizing.} A 16-vCPU guest that
        used two becomes a 16-vCPU instance billed for sixteen. The
        right-sizing of \chref{cpumem} is worth more in the cloud, because
        waste is now a monthly invoice.
  \item \textbf{Benchmarking on a burstable instance.} You are measuring credit
        balance, not performance.
  \item \textbf{Ignoring egress until the exit.} Price it at design time.
  \item \textbf{Assuming multi-cloud buys availability.} It buys the
        intersection of two feature sets, two operational models and two sets
        of outages. It buys leverage and sometimes compliance; availability is
        usually cheaper within one provider across zones.
  \item \textbf{Treating the shared responsibility diagram as marketing.} It is
        the contract, and it is where the incidents are.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lab}[One Workload, Three Clouds]
Launches the same instance on AWS, Azure and GCP free tiers, compares what each
exposes, and tears everything down. \textbf{Step 7 is not optional:} a forgotten
instance bills until somebody notices. Expect fifty minutes.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# ch14_threeclouds.sh -- one guest on three providers, then delete.
set -euo pipefail

# --- 1. Authenticate to whichever accounts you have. -------------------
aws sts get-caller-identity   2>/dev/null || echo "no aws credentials"
az account show --query name  2>/dev/null || echo "no azure login"
gcloud config get-value project 2>/dev/null || echo "no gcp project"

# --- 2. AWS: a free-tier instance. ------------------------------------
AMI=$(aws ssm get-parameters --names \
  /aws/service/ami-amazon-linux-latest/al2023-ami-kernel-6.1-x86_64 \
  --query 'Parameters[0].Value' --output text)
aws ec2 run-instances --image-id "$AMI" --instance-type t3.micro \
  --count 1 --tag-specifications \
  'ResourceType=instance,Tags=[{Key=Name,Value=vss-lab}]' \
  --query 'Instances[0].InstanceId' --output text | tee aws-id.txt

# --- 3. Azure: the same shape. ----------------------------------------
az group create -n vss-lab -l eastus -o none
az vm create -g vss-lab -n vss1 --image Ubuntu2204 --size Standard_B1s \
  --generate-ssh-keys --public-ip-sku Basic -o table

# --- 4. GCP: likewise. ------------------------------------------------
gcloud compute instances create vss1 --machine-type=e2-micro \
  --zone=us-central1-a --image-family=debian-12 \
  --image-project=debian-cloud

# --- 5. Ask each guest what it is running on. -------------------------
# Every provider exposes a metadata service on 169.254.169.254. The
# answers show you the hypervisor of Chapter 5 underneath all three.
cat <<'PROBE' > probe.sh
systemd-detect-virt
sudo dmidecode -s system-manufacturer 2>/dev/null
curl -s -H 'Metadata-Flavor: Google' \
  169.254.169.254/computeMetadata/v1/instance/machine-type 2>/dev/null
curl -s 169.254.169.254/latest/meta-data/instance-type 2>/dev/null
PROBE
echo "copy probe.sh to each instance and run it"

# --- 6. What each one costs, from the provider's own API. -------------
aws pricing get-products --service-code AmazonEC2 --region us-east-1 \
  --filters 'Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=instanceType,Value=m7i.xlarge' \
            'Type=TERM_MATCH,Field=location,Value=US East (N. Virginia)' \
  --max-results 1 --query 'PriceList[0]' --output text 2>/dev/null \
  | head -c 400 || echo "needs the pricing:GetProducts permission"

# --- 7. TEAR DOWN. Do this even if a step above failed. ---------------
aws ec2 terminate-instances --instance-ids "$(cat aws-id.txt)" \
  --query 'TerminatingInstances[0].CurrentState.Name' --output text || true
az group delete -n vss-lab --yes --no-wait || true
gcloud compute instances delete vss1 --zone=us-central1-a --quiet || true

# --- 8. Confirm nothing survives. -------------------------------------
aws ec2 describe-instances --filters 'Name=tag:Name,Values=vss-lab' \
  'Name=instance-state-name,Values=running,pending' \
  --query 'Reservations[].Instances[].InstanceId' --output text
az group list --query "[?name=='vss-lab'].name" -o tsv
gcloud compute instances list --filter='name=vss1' --format='value(name)'
# All three should print nothing.
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What to notice.} Step 5 is the point of the whole chapter: three
providers, three billing models, three APIs --- and underneath, the same
hypervisor, the same virtio devices and the same overlay network you built by
hand in \chref{network}. The cloud is this book, rented.
\end{lab}

\begin{checkpoint}
\begin{tightnum}
  \item A university runs a self-service vSphere portal where departments
        provision guests themselves and are charged internally per
        guest-month. Test it against the five NIST characteristics and say
        which, if any, it fails.
  \item A workload runs 10 hours a day, 5 days a week. Using the worked
        example's hourly rate, compute its annual on-demand cost, and say why
        a three-year reservation would probably be a mistake.
  \item An object store containing student records was found publicly
        readable. Which side of the shared responsibility line is this on, and
        what would you change to stop it recurring?
\end{tightnum}
\end{checkpoint}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item NIST's five characteristics are a usable test. Most ``private clouds''
        fail on elasticity, because capacity cannot be returned.
  \item IaaS, PaaS, SaaS and FaaS differ only in where the responsibility line
        falls. It only ever moves upward, and everything below it is also
        something you can no longer tune or debug.
  \item Serverless is somebody else's server started in 125 ms. It is
        economically possible because of \chref{lightweight}.
  \item Spread across availability zones for availability, across regions for
        disaster recovery and data residency.
  \item On demand, the same workload cost 61\% more than owning the hardware;
        with a three-year commitment it cost 21\% less. The purchasing
        decision mattered more than the platform.
  \item Egress is charged and ingress is not. That asymmetry is what lock-in
        actually is; price it at design time.
  \item You are responsible for security \emph{in} the cloud. Nearly every
        major breach has been on the customer's side of that line.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}
