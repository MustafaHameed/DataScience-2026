\section{One Host Is Not a Small Fleet}

Everything in Parts~II and~III was about one machine. A hypervisor on its own
is a complete, usable product --- and it stops being enough at about the third
host, for reasons that are all variations on the same one: a decision that was
obvious with one host has no obvious answer with twenty.

\begin{conceptbox}[Placement Is the Capability That Pays for the Platform]
The feature that justifies a management layer commercially is automated
placement --- VMware calls it DRS, and the others have equivalents.

\smallskip
The cluster continuously measures load and migrates guests between hosts to
balance it. That turns twenty hosts from twenty capacity decisions into one,
and it makes maintenance routine: put a host in maintenance mode and its guests
leave by themselves.

\smallskip
It also changes the sizing arithmetic of \chref{cpumem}. A single host must be
sized for the peak of everything on it. A balanced cluster need only be sized
for the peak of the \emph{aggregate}, and peaks that do not coincide stop
needing separate headroom --- commonly 15--25\% less hardware for the same
service.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{alertbox}[OpenStack is not in the same category as the others]
It is routinely compared with vCenter and the comparison misleads in both
directions.

\smallskip
vCenter manages \emph{your} servers for \emph{your} staff. OpenStack provides a
\emph{cloud} --- the five NIST characteristics of \chref{cloud}, including
tenants, quotas, metering and an API contract you offer to others. It is
roughly a dozen interacting services, and that is because the problem is
bigger, not because it is badly engineered.

\smallskip
The practical consequence: OpenStack rewards an organisation with a dedicated
platform team and punishes one without. Several well-known deployments were
abandoned for exactly this reason. If what you need is to run two hundred
guests for your own department, it is the wrong tool and Proxmox or oVirt is
the right one.
\end{alertbox}

\section{Infrastructure as Code}

A console is fine for one change and hopeless as a system of record. Clicking
through a wizard leaves nothing behind that says what was intended, so six
months later nobody can say whether a setting was deliberate.

\term{Infrastructure as code} makes the declaration the artefact. The
definition lives in version control, is reviewed like any change, and the tool
makes reality match it --- the same reconciliation idea as \chref{kubernetes},
applied to infrastructure.

\begin{definitionbox}[Declarative, Idempotent, and the State File]
A declarative tool is given the desired end state, not the steps. Terraform's
loop is \emph{plan} --- compare desired with actual and show the difference ---
then \emph{apply}.

\smallskip
\term{Idempotent} means applying the same definition twice changes nothing the
second time. This is what makes the tool safe to run on a schedule, and it is
the property a shell script of \texttt{virsh} commands does not have.

\smallskip
To compare desired with actual, the tool must know which real objects it
created. That is the \term{state file}, and it is the part that bites: it is
authoritative, it contains secrets, and two people applying at once will
corrupt it. Keep it in remote storage with locking, never in the repository.
\end{definitionbox}

\begin{worked}[What Automation Is Worth, and When It Is Not]
A team provisions guests by hand: 45 minutes each, including the ticket, the
console work and the follow-up corrections. They provision about 12 a month and
reconfigure about 30. Decide whether to adopt Terraform.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 1 --- the current cost.}
\[ 12 \times 45 = 540 \text{ min} = 9 \text{ h a month on new guests} \]
Reconfigurations average 20 minutes:
\[ 30 \times 20 = 600 \text{ min} = 10 \text{ h a month} \]
\[ \text{total} = 19 \text{ h a month} = \mathbf{228 \text{ h a year}} \]

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 2 --- the cost after.} Writing the module takes 3 days, 24 hours,
once. A new guest is then 5 minutes of editing and review; a reconfiguration is
3 minutes:
\[ 12 \times 5 + 30 \times 3 = 60 + 90 = 150 \text{ min} = 2.5
   \text{ h a month} \]
\[ = 30 \text{ h a year, plus } 24 \text{ h to build} \]

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 3 --- first-year saving.}
\[ 228 - (30 + 24) = \mathbf{174 \text{ hours}} \]
and 198 hours in every year after. The build pays for itself in about seven
weeks.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 4 --- the benefit that is not in the arithmetic.} The 45 minutes
included ``follow-up corrections'' --- guests built slightly differently from
each other, discovered later. Those corrections do not merely take time; they
are the \emph{drift} of the next section, and the saving from eliminating them
is larger than the 174 hours and much harder to measure.

\smallskip
\textbf{Step 5 --- and the honest case against.} Change the inputs and the
answer inverts. A team provisioning \emph{one} guest a month saves
$12 \times 40 = 8$ hours a year against a 24-hour build and an ongoing
maintenance burden --- a clear loss. Automation pays at volume and at
repetition, and a tool adopted below that threshold is a liability maintained
by someone who uses it too rarely to remember it.

\smallskip
State the threshold explicitly before adopting: \emph{how many of these do we
do, and how alike are they?}
\end{worked}

\begin{conceptbox}[cloud-init: the last mile, every boot]
A golden image must be identical for every guest, and every guest must be
different --- different hostname, address, keys, role. \term{cloud-init}
resolves the contradiction: it runs on first boot, reads \term{user data} from
the platform's metadata service, and applies it.

\smallskip
It is why the Chapter~1 lab could hand a downloaded cloud image a password in
a two-line YAML file and have it work. Every cloud in \chref{cloud} supports it,
and so do libvirt, Proxmox and VMware, which is why one image can be built once
and used everywhere.

\smallskip
The rule that follows: \emph{anything that differs between guests belongs in
user data, and anything that is the same belongs in the image.} Put a hostname
in the image and you will clone a hostname conflict; put a patch in user data
and you will apply it at every boot for ever.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Drift}

\term{Configuration drift} is the gap between what a system is supposed to be
and what it has become: a package installed to debug something at 2 a.m., a
limit raised and never recorded, a firewall rule added for a test. Each is
small and reasonable. After two years no two servers are alike and nobody can
rebuild any of them.

Two responses, and they are genuinely different philosophies.

\begin{conceptbox}[Converge, or Replace]
\textbf{Convergent management} --- Ansible, Puppet, Chef --- re-applies the
desired configuration periodically, correcting what has drifted. It works on
long-lived systems, including ones you cannot rebuild, and it is the only
option for an estate that already exists. Its weakness is that it corrects only
what it is told to check: an unmanaged file stays drifted for ever.

\smallskip
\textbf{Immutable infrastructure} --- never change a running system. To patch
it, build a new image and replace the instance. Drift becomes structurally
impossible, because nothing lives long enough to drift, and every running
system is exactly some reviewed version. The cost is that it demands the whole
pipeline, fast instantiation and workloads that tolerate replacement ---
stateless services, or state kept deliberately outside.

\smallskip
Most real estates are both: immutable for the application tier, convergent for
the database servers and the appliances that cannot be rebuilt. Knowing which
regime a given machine is under --- and writing it down --- matters more than
which one you prefer.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Keeping the state file in the repository.} It holds secrets and
        it must be locked. Remote backend with locking, always.
  \item \textbf{Running \texttt{apply} without reading \texttt{plan}.} The plan
        is the review. Reading it is the control that stops a one-character
        change destroying a database.
  \item \textbf{Editing a golden image in place.} Then it is not a version and
        you cannot say what any guest was built from.
  \item \textbf{Putting per-guest settings in the image.} Clone it and you
        clone the hostname and the SSH host key.
  \item \textbf{Adopting automation below its threshold.} At one guest a month
        the tool costs more than it saves and is forgotten between uses.
  \item \textbf{Fixing a drifted server by hand and closing the ticket.} The
        fix now exists in one place and nowhere else; the next rebuild loses
        it.
  \item \textbf{Choosing OpenStack to manage a departmental estate.} It is a
        cloud platform. Match the tool to the problem's size.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{lab}[Declaring a Small Estate]
Builds an image with a pipeline, then provisions and reconfigures guests
declaratively with Terraform against libvirt. Expect forty-five minutes.

\begin{lstlisting}[language=bash]
#!/usr/bin/env bash
# ch16_iac.sh -- an image pipeline and declarative provisioning.
set -euo pipefail
mkdir -p ~/vsiac && cd ~/vsiac

# --- 1. The build stage: one patched image, made reproducibly. --------
# Packer would do this in a pipeline; here, the same steps by hand.
BASE=https://cloud-images.ubuntu.com/jammy/current
wget -nc -O base.img "$BASE/jammy-server-cloudimg-amd64.img"
cp base.img golden-2026-10.qcow2
sudo virt-customize -a golden-2026-10.qcow2 \
  --update --install qemu-guest-agent,chrony \
  --run-command 'systemctl enable qemu-guest-agent' \
  --truncate /etc/machine-id          # or every clone shares an identity
qemu-img info golden-2026-10.qcow2 | head -4

# --- 2. The publish stage: versioned, and never edited again. ---------
sudo mkdir -p /var/lib/libvirt/images/templates
sudo cp golden-2026-10.qcow2 /var/lib/libvirt/images/templates/
sudo chmod 444 /var/lib/libvirt/images/templates/golden-2026-10.qcow2
ls -l /var/lib/libvirt/images/templates/

# --- 3. Declare the estate. ------------------------------------------
cat > main.tf <<'TF'
terraform {
  required_providers {
    libvirt = { source = "dmacvicar/libvirt", version = "~> 0.8" }
  }
}
provider "libvirt" { uri = "qemu:///system" }

variable "guests" {
  type    = map(object({ memory = number, vcpu = number }))
  default = {
    web1 = { memory = 1024, vcpu = 1 }
    web2 = { memory = 1024, vcpu = 1 }
    db1  = { memory = 2048, vcpu = 2 }
  }
}

resource "libvirt_volume" "disk" {
  for_each       = var.guests
  name           = "${each.key}.qcow2"
  base_volume_id = "/var/lib/libvirt/images/templates/golden-2026-10.qcow2"
  size           = 10737418240
}

resource "libvirt_cloudinit_disk" "init" {
  for_each  = var.guests
  name      = "${each.key}-init.iso"
  user_data = <<EOT
#cloud-config
hostname: ${each.key}
password: vss2026
chpasswd: { expire: False }
ssh_pwauth: True
EOT
}

resource "libvirt_domain" "vm" {
  for_each  = var.guests
  name      = each.key
  memory    = each.value.memory
  vcpu      = each.value.vcpu
  cloudinit = libvirt_cloudinit_disk.init[each.key].id
  disk { volume_id = libvirt_volume.disk[each.key].id }
  network_interface { network_name = "default" }
  console { type = "pty", target_port = "0" }
}
TF

# --- 4. Plan first. The plan IS the review. --------------------------
terraform init -input=false
terraform plan -out=tfplan | tail -20

# --- 5. Apply, and then prove idempotence. ---------------------------
terraform apply -auto-approve tfplan
sudo virsh list --all
terraform plan | tail -3        # "No changes" -- this is the property
                                # a shell script of virsh does not have.

# --- 6. Reconfigure declaratively: change one number. ----------------
sed -i 's/db1  = { memory = 2048/db1  = { memory = 4096/' main.tf
terraform plan | grep -E 'memory|will be'
terraform apply -auto-approve

# --- 7. Create drift by hand, then watch the tool notice. ------------
sudo virsh setmaxmem web1 3145728 --config
sudo virsh setmem web1 3145728 --config
terraform plan | tail -8        # the difference, reported

# --- 8. Tear the whole estate down with one command. -----------------
terraform destroy -auto-approve
sudo virsh list --all
\end{lstlisting}

\textbf{What to notice.} Step 5's second \texttt{plan} is the whole argument:
running it again does nothing, so it is safe to run on a schedule. Step 7 is
the other half --- the tool can tell you the estate has been changed behind its
back, which no console can.
\end{lab}

\begin{checkpoint}
\begin{tightnum}
  \item A team provisions 3 guests a month at 50 minutes each and reconfigures
        8 at 25 minutes. A Terraform module would take 4 days and reduce these
        to 5 and 3 minutes. Work out the first-year saving and say whether you
        would adopt it.
  \item Explain what belongs in a golden image and what belongs in cloud-init
        user data, with one example of each being put in the wrong place and
        the consequence.
  \item A database server cannot be rebuilt because its configuration was never
        captured. Which drift strategy applies to it, and what would you do to
        change that over the next year?
\end{tightnum}
\end{checkpoint}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item A management layer exists because placement, evacuation, failure
        recovery, access control and consistency have no obvious answers at
        twenty hosts. Automated placement is what pays for it.
  \item vCenter, SCVMM, oVirt and Proxmox manage \emph{servers}; OpenStack
        provides a \emph{cloud}. Matching the tool to the size of the problem
        matters more than the feature comparison.
  \item Infrastructure as code makes the declaration the artefact: declarative,
        idempotent, reviewed. The state file is authoritative, holds secrets
        and must be locked.
  \item Automation pays at volume and repetition. Below that threshold it costs
        more than it saves --- state the threshold before adopting.
  \item Build, publish, instantiate. Changes go back to the image; anything
        differing between guests goes in cloud-init user data.
  \item Drift is corrected by convergence or made impossible by immutability.
        Most estates run both, and knowing which applies to a machine matters
        more than the preference.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}

\begin{reviewq}
  \item \textbf{(MCQ)} Terraform's state file: (a)~is a log of past actions;
        (b)~records which real objects the tool created, and must be locked;
        (c)~is optional; (d)~belongs in version control.
  \item \textbf{(MCQ)} Idempotence means: (a)~the tool runs quickly;
        (b)~applying the same definition twice changes nothing the second time;
        (c)~changes can be rolled back; (d)~the configuration is encrypted.
  \item \textbf{(Short)} Explain how automated placement reduces the hardware a
        cluster needs, referring to \chref{cpumem}.
  \item \textbf{(Short)} Give the three stages of the image pipeline and state
        what must never be done between them.
  \item \textbf{(Short)} Contrast convergent and immutable approaches to drift,
        and give a workload suited to each.
  \item \textbf{(Applied)} A department running 60 guests on three standalone
        ESXi hosts with no vCenter asks what they are missing. Write the answer,
        in priority order, with the operational consequence of each gap and a
        free alternative where one exists.
  \item \textbf{(Applied)} An estate has 200 guests, no image pipeline and no
        record of how any was built. Design a twelve-month plan to reach a
        state where any guest can be rebuilt from a reviewed definition,
        saying what you would do first and why.
\end{reviewq}
