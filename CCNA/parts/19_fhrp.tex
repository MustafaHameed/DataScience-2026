\section{The problem}

Every host is configured with one default gateway address. If the router holding
that address fails, every host on the subnet is cut off --- and no routing
protocol helps, because the hosts are not running one. They have a single static
address and no way to learn a new one quickly.

\begin{conceptbox}[The solution: a gateway address that is not tied to a router]
Two routers share a \term{virtual IP address} and a \term{virtual MAC address}.
Hosts are configured with the virtual IP as their gateway and never know that
two routers exist. One router is elected to answer for it; the other waits.

When the active router fails, the standby takes over \emph{both} the virtual IP
and the virtual MAC. Because the MAC does not change, the hosts' ARP caches stay
valid and no host has to do anything at all. That is the whole trick, and it is
why the virtual MAC matters as much as the virtual IP.
\end{conceptbox}

\begin{alertbox}[The preemption default is the difference that catches people]
HSRP does \textbf{not} preempt by default. Configure a router with priority 150,
reload it, and when it comes back the other router --- priority 100 --- keeps the
active role. Your carefully planned primary is now the standby, and everything
still works, so nobody notices until the second failure.

VRRP preempts by default, so it behaves the way most people expect.

If you want a specific router to be primary, configure \cmd{standby 10 preempt}
explicitly. This is the single most commonly missed HSRP command.
\end{alertbox}

\section{Configuring HSRP}

\begin{config}{HSRP on two multilayer switches, VLAN 10}
! ---------- CORE1: the intended primary ----------
interface Vlan10
 ip address 192.168.10.2 255.255.255.0
 ! The virtual IP -- this is what hosts use as their gateway.
 standby 10 ip 192.168.10.1
 ! Higher priority wins the election.
 standby 10 priority 150
 ! Without this, CORE1 will NOT take the role back after a reload.
 standby 10 preempt
 ! Give up the role if the uplink dies -- otherwise CORE1 stays
 ! active while having no path to anywhere.
 standby 10 track GigabitEthernet0/24 60
 standby 10 name VLAN10-GW
!
! ---------- CORE2: the standby ----------
interface Vlan10
 ip address 192.168.10.3 255.255.255.0
 standby 10 ip 192.168.10.1
 standby 10 priority 100
 standby 10 preempt
\end{config}

\begin{conceptbox}[Tracking, and why it is not optional]
Without tracking, the active router keeps the role as long as its own VLAN
interface is up. If its \emph{uplink} fails, it is still active, still answering
for the gateway, and has nowhere to send the traffic --- a black hole that the
standby router cannot detect, because from its point of view the active router is
perfectly healthy.

\cmd{standby 10 track Gi0/24 60} decrements the priority by 60 when that
interface goes down: 150 becomes 90, which is below CORE2's 100, so CORE2
preempts and takes over. The decrement must be large enough to cross the gap
between the two priorities, or nothing happens.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Interpreting the status}

\begin{verify}{CORE1}{show standby brief}
                     P indicates configured to preempt.
                     |
Interface   Grp  Pri P State    Active          Standby         Virtual IP
Vl10        10   150 P Active   local           192.168.10.3    192.168.10.1
Vl20        20   100 P Standby  192.168.10.3    local           192.168.20.1
\end{verify}

Read it column by column. \cmd{Pri} is this router's priority. \cmd{P} means
preemption is configured --- its absence is the fault described above.
\cmd{State} is the role. \cmd{Active} and \cmd{Standby} name who holds each role,
where \cmd{local} means this router.

Note that VLAN 10 and VLAN 20 have opposite roles here. That is deliberate load
sharing: half the VLANs are active on each core switch, so both boxes carry
traffic instead of one sitting idle.

\begin{verify}{CORE1}{show standby Vlan10}
Vlan10 - Group 10
  State is Active
    2 state changes, last state change 04:12:19
  Virtual IP address is 192.168.10.1
  Active virtual MAC address is 0000.0c07.ac0a
    Local virtual MAC address is 0000.0c07.ac0a (v1 default)
  Hello time 3 sec, hold time 10 sec
  Preemption enabled
  Active router is local
  Standby router is 192.168.10.3, priority 100 (expires in 8.048 sec)
  Priority 150 (configured 150)
    Track interface GigabitEthernet0/24 state Up decrement 60
\end{verify}

Two lines to check first: \cmd{Priority 150 (configured 150)} --- if the two
numbers differ, a tracked interface is down and has decremented it --- and
\cmd{Preemption enabled}.

Interface          Grp Pri Time    Own Pre State   Master addr     Group addr
Vl30               30  110 3570     Y  Y   Master  192.168.30.2    192.168.30.1
\end{verify}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Forgetting \cmd{preempt} on HSRP.} The intended primary never
        takes the role back.
  \item \textbf{Mismatched group numbers.} Two routers with different group
        numbers on the same VLAN both become active, each for its own group. The
        symptom is intermittent connectivity as hosts ARP for a virtual IP that
        two devices answer.
  \item \textbf{Mismatched virtual IPs.} Same result.
  \item \textbf{A track decrement too small to matter.} 150 with a decrement of
        10 still beats 100. Size the decrement against the gap.
  \item \textbf{Configuring the virtual IP as a router's real address in HSRP.}
        Not allowed. VRRP permits it; HSRP does not.
  \item \textbf{Assuming the gateway works because the routers are up.} Test by
        pinging the \emph{virtual} address from a host, then failing the active
        router and pinging again.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{breakfix}[Both routers say they are active.]
Users on VLAN 10 report intermittent loss. Both core switches report the active
role for group 10.

\begin{verify}{CORE1}{show standby brief}
Interface   Grp  Pri P State    Active          Standby         Virtual IP
Vl10        10   150 P Active   local           unknown         192.168.10.1
\end{verify}

\begin{verify}{CORE2}{show standby brief}
Interface   Grp  Pri P State    Active          Standby         Virtual IP
Vl10        11   100 P Active   local           unknown         192.168.10.1
\end{verify}

\textbf{Diagnosis.} Look at the \cmd{Grp} column: CORE1 is in group \textbf{10},
CORE2 in group \textbf{11}. They are not peers at all --- they are two independent
HSRP groups that happen to share a virtual IP address. Each sees no partner, so
each elects itself active, and each answers ARP for \cmd{192.168.10.1} with a
\emph{different} virtual MAC (\cmd{0000.0c07.ac0a} against
\cmd{0000.0c07.ac0b}). Hosts get whichever reply arrives first, and traffic
splits unpredictably between two routers.

The giveaway is \cmd{Standby unknown} on both sides. A healthy pair always names
its partner.

\textbf{Fix.} Correct the group number on CORE2 to 10. Within a hello interval the
two will see each other, the priorities decide, and one becomes standby. Confirm
that each side names the other in the \cmd{Standby} column, and that only one
virtual MAC is now in use.
\end{breakfix}

\begin{lab}{Fail a gateway and watch it survive}{Packet Tracer}
\textbf{Topology.} Two multilayer switches as redundant gateways for VLANs 10 and
20; an access switch; two PCs.

\begin{tasks}
  \item Address both cores in VLAN 10 and configure HSRP group 10 with virtual IP
        \cmd{192.168.10.1}, CORE1 at priority 150 with preempt.
  \item Point the PCs at the virtual IP and confirm they can reach the rest of the
        network. Record the ARP entry a PC holds for its gateway --- note the
        virtual MAC.
  \item Run \cmd{show standby brief} on both. Identify active, standby and the
        preempt flag.
  \item Start a continuous ping from a PC, then shut CORE1's VLAN 10 interface.
        Count how many pings are lost and confirm the PC's ARP entry has
        \emph{not} changed.
  \item Bring CORE1 back and confirm it resumes the active role. Then remove
        \cmd{preempt}, repeat, and observe that it does not.
  \item Configure tracking on CORE1's uplink with a decrement of 60. Shut the
        uplink and confirm failover; use a decrement of 10 instead and confirm it
        does not.
  \item Configure VLAN 20 with CORE2 as primary, so the two share the load.
  \item \textbf{Extension.} Repeat the first four steps with VRRP on a third VLAN
        and note the difference in default preemption behaviour.
\end{tasks}
\end{lab}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item Hosts have one static gateway address, so redundancy has to be provided
        below them: two routers share a virtual IP \emph{and} a virtual MAC.
  \item HSRP is Cisco, uses active and standby, and \textbf{does not preempt by
        default}. VRRP is standard, uses master and backup, and does.
  \item Priority decides; preemption decides whether the better router takes the
        role back.
  \item Track the uplink, with a decrement large enough to cross the priority
        gap, or the active router can become a black hole.
  \item Interpret \cmd{show standby brief} by columns: priority, the \cmd{P}
        flag, state, and who is named as partner. \cmd{Standby unknown} means the
        two are not peering.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}
