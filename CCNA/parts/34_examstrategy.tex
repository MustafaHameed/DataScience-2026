\chapter{Exam Strategy}\label{ch:examstrategy}

\begin{outcomes}
By the end of this chapter you will be able to:
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Recognise} each question type and know what it demands of you.
  \item \textbf{Budget} 120 minutes across a paper you cannot navigate backwards
        through.
  \item \textbf{Read} a question for its qualifiers before answering it.
  \item \textbf{Plan} the final month of revision against the verb distribution
        rather than against the chapter list.
\end{tight}
\end{outcomes}

\begin{alertbox}[What this chapter can and cannot tell you]
The 120-minute duration and the domain weights come from Cisco's published exam
description and are reliable.

\textbf{Cisco does not publish the number of questions, and does not publish a
passing score for this exam.} Scores are scaled, and the cut score can change
between forms. Anyone quoting you an exact question count or an exact percentage
is repeating hearsay, and this book will not add to it.

What follows is therefore built on the facts Cisco does publish plus the
arithmetic of a fixed two hours. Before you book, work through Cisco's own free
exam tutorial so that the interface is not new on the day, and re-read the
current exam policies --- they change, and this chapter does not update itself.
\end{alertbox}

\section{The constraint that shapes everything}

\begin{alertbox}[You cannot go back]
Cisco's exam engine does not let you return to a question once you have moved
past it. There is no review screen, no flag-for-later, no second pass.

Every habit you built sitting university papers --- skim first, do the easy ones,
come back to the hard ones --- is unavailable. Sitting this exam as though it
were a normal paper is the most expensive mistake available to you, and it is
entirely avoidable.

Three consequences follow, and they are the whole of exam strategy here:

\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Every question must be answered when you meet it.} Never leave
        one blank intending to return. You cannot.
  \item \textbf{Never leave one blank at all.} There is no negative marking. An
        educated guess is worth strictly more than a blank.
  \item \textbf{A question you dwell on is stealing time from questions you have
        not seen yet}, and you have no way to know what they are worth.
\end{tight}
\end{alertbox}

\begin{examnote}[The AI-evaluation questions are not confined to domain 5]
Cisco's exam description says you ``may be required to evaluate output and
recommendations from agentic AI and digital network assistants to support network
operations and troubleshooting activities.''

Read that as: a question anywhere in the paper may show you an assistant's
suggested diagnosis or configuration and ask whether it is right. The skill is
the one from \chref{ai} --- a plausible, fluent, confidently wrong answer is the
characteristic failure --- combined with the actual technical knowledge to spot
it. Treat such output exactly as you would a suggestion from a colleague you do
not know: check it against what the exhibit shows.
\end{examnote}

\section{Budgeting 120 minutes}

You do not know the question count, but you do know the shape: a majority of
short questions and a small number of simulations that cost ten to fifteen
minutes each.

\begin{longtable}{@{}L{4.6cm}L{10.4cm}@{}}
\toprule
\rowcolor{primary!15}
\textbf{Rule} & \textbf{Why} \\
\midrule
\endhead
\textbf{60 to 75 seconds} for a standard question &
Enough to read carefully and eliminate. If you are past 90 seconds you are
already losing \\
\rowcolor{lightbg}
\textbf{Answer and move} at 90 seconds &
Pick the best remaining option and go. The question you have not seen may be one
you would have answered in twenty seconds \\
\textbf{10 to 15 minutes} for a simulation &
They involve typing, and typing is slow. Budget for them or they take the time
from everything else \\
\rowcolor{lightbg}
\textbf{Check the clock at fixed points}, not continuously &
Roughly every twenty minutes. Continuous clock-watching costs both time and
composure \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}

\begin{conceptbox}[Simulations: do them properly when you meet them]
Because you cannot come back, a simulation cannot be deferred. Skimping on it to
save time is a false economy --- it is worth several ordinary questions.

\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Read the whole task first.} All of it. Then plan the commands
        before typing any.
  \item \textbf{Configure exactly what is asked and nothing else.} Extra work
        risks breaking a check.
  \item \textbf{Verify with \cmd{show} before you leave.} The habit from every
        chapter of this book applies here and takes thirty seconds.
  \item \textbf{Save the configuration if the task says to} --- and consider it
        even when it does not. \cmd{copy running-config startup-config} costs
        nothing (\chref{ansible} makes the same point about \cmd{save\_when}).
  \item \textbf{If you cannot complete it, complete part of it.} A partially
        configured task scores more than an untouched one.
\end{tight}
\end{conceptbox}

\section{Reading the question}

Most wrong answers on questions candidates \emph{knew} come from misreading, and
the misreadings are predictable.

\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Qualifiers.} ``BEST'', ``MOST likely'', ``FIRST'', ``LEAST''. More
        than one option is often defensible; the qualifier picks between them.
        ``What should you check first'' has a different answer from ``what is the
        cause''.
  \item \textbf{Negatives.} ``Which is \emph{not} true'', ``which would
        \emph{fail}''. Slow down; these are where a fluent reader loses marks.
  \item \textbf{The count.} ``Choose two'' is an instruction, not a hint.
  \item \textbf{The scenario's constraints.} ``Without interrupting existing
        traffic'' or ``on IOS XE'' is there to eliminate options, and it is
        usually the whole question --- \cmd{switchport trunk allowed vlan 50}
        against \cmd{... vlan add 50} is exactly this (\chref{trunking}).
  \item \textbf{Exhibit first or question first?} \textbf{Question first},
        always. Then read the exhibit for what you now know you need.
\end{tight}

\begin{alertbox}[Answer the question that was asked]
The commonest self-inflicted error is answering a slightly different, more
interesting question --- explaining the cause when asked for the next diagnostic
step, or giving the best long-term design when asked what restores service now.

Before selecting, re-read the interrogative. It takes two seconds.
\end{alertbox}

\begin{conceptbox}[Subnetting must be automatic, not calculable]
It appears inside other questions --- an ACL wildcard, a summary route, whether
two addresses are on the same subnet --- where it is not the point of the
question but stands between you and the answer.

If you are working it out on paper, it is costing you thirty seconds every time
it appears. Drill it until the common masks are recall rather than arithmetic.
Appendix~\ref{app:subnetting} exists for this.
\end{conceptbox}

\section{On the day}

\begin{tight}
  \item Arrive early with the identification the test centre requires; check
        their rules in advance, and if sitting online, test the machine and the
        room beforehand.
  \item Expect a whiteboard or its digital equivalent. Before the clock matters,
        consider writing down what you know you forget --- the subnet boundary
        table, the eight syslog severities, the STP port states.
  \item Do the first question properly. Settling into a rhythm early is worth
        more than the two minutes it costs.
  \item If a question is genuinely unfamiliar: eliminate, choose, move. You will
        not think of it later, and later does not exist here.
  \item If you finish with time in hand, you cannot revisit anything --- so
        there is nothing to do but finish. Spend the time on the questions in
        front of you instead of hurrying to that point.
\end{tight}

\begin{pitfall}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{Planning to come back to it.} You cannot.
  \item \textbf{Leaving anything blank.} No negative marking.
  \item \textbf{Reading the exhibit before the question.}
  \item \textbf{Missing ``choose two''.}
  \item \textbf{Rushing a simulation} to protect time for questions you have not
        seen.
  \item \textbf{Not verifying a simulation} with a \cmd{show} command.
  \item \textbf{Revising by re-reading} when 55\% of the paper is hands-on.
  \item \textbf{Learning the interface on the day.} Take the tutorial.
  \item \textbf{Trusting a quoted pass mark or question count.} Cisco publishes
        neither.
\end{tight}
\end{pitfall}

\begin{breakfix}[A candidate who knew the material and ran out of time.]
A candidate rebuilds every lab in this book, works every Break \& Fix, and scores
well on practice tests. They fail. Their time log, reconstructed afterwards,
looks like this.

\begin{verify}{Reconstructed}{time log}
00:00 - 00:34   First 18 questions. Comfortable, roughly 110 sec each.
00:34 - 00:48   One OSPF question. Recognised it, wanted to be sure,
                worked the DR election through on the whiteboard.
00:48 - 01:12   Next 12 questions, hurrying now, roughly 120 sec each.
01:12 - 01:31   First simulation. Completed, not verified.
01:31 - 01:52   Next 9 questions. Guessing on several.
01:52 - 02:00   Second simulation reached with 8 minutes left.
                Partially configured. Not saved.
\end{verify}

\textbf{Diagnosis.} The knowledge was there. Three decisions spent it.

\textbf{The 14 minutes at 00:34.} One question, already answered correctly in
their head, verified at the cost of roughly twelve other questions' worth of
time. The instinct --- be certain before committing, because you cannot come back
--- is understandable and exactly backwards: you cannot come back, so the cost of
over-investing is unrecoverable too.

\textbf{The hurrying that followed.} Every question from 00:48 onward was
answered under self-inflicted pressure created fourteen minutes earlier. Time
lost early is not repaid; it is paid for by everything after it.

\textbf{The second simulation.} Reached with eight minutes left because of the
first two decisions, and worth several times an ordinary question. Partially
configured and not saved --- so much of the work that was done scored nothing.

\textbf{What should have happened.} At 00:44 --- ten minutes into that OSPF
question --- the correct move was to select the best answer available and go. It
was probably right. Even if it was wrong, one mark is cheaper than twelve.

\textbf{The rule to take in with you.} \textbf{No single question is worth more
than about ninety seconds.} Not the one you nearly know, not the one you are
annoyed by, not the one you could definitely get with a little longer. Set that
limit before you sit down, because you will not set it fairly at 00:40 with a
half-solved problem in front of you.
\end{breakfix}

\begin{lab}{A full-length timed rehearsal}{Your own material, a timer, and no interruptions}
\textbf{Why this is the last lab in the book.} You have practised the material.
You have not practised the \emph{conditions} --- two hours, no going back, no
notes, no pausing. Those are a separate skill and they are what the Break \& Fix
above is about.

\begin{tasks}
  \item Assemble a paper: about 90 questions from the review sections of this
        book, weighted to the domain percentages --- roughly a quarter each from
        Parts I and II, a fifth each from Parts III and IV, a tenth from Part~V.
  \item Add two configuration tasks from the labs, to stand in for simulations.
  \item \textbf{Set the rules and keep them.} 120 minutes. No notes. \textbf{No
        returning to a previous question} --- cover them, or have someone else
        administer it. This constraint is the entire point of the exercise.
  \item Sit it in one block, at the time of day you have booked the real exam.
  \item Record a timestamp every ten questions. Do not analyse during.
  \item \textbf{Afterwards, mark the timing before you mark the answers.} Find
        every question that took more than 90 seconds and count what they cost
        collectively. This number is usually a surprise.
  \item Now mark the answers. Separate the wrong ones into \emph{did not know}
        and \emph{knew but misread or rushed}. They need completely different
        remedies, and the second pile is usually bigger than expected.
  \item For every ``did not know'', find the chapter and redo its lab --- not its
        summary.
  \item \textbf{Repeat two weeks later} with a different selection. Compare the
        timing profile, not just the score. Improvement in timing is the better
        predictor.
\end{tasks}
\end{lab}

\begin{chaptersummary}
\begin{tight}
  \item \textbf{You cannot return to a question.} Answer everything when you meet
        it, never leave a blank, and never plan to come back.
  \item Roughly 60--75 seconds per standard question; hard stop at 90. Budget
        10--15 minutes for each simulation.
  \item No single question is worth more than ninety seconds. Time lost early is
        never repaid.
  \item Question before exhibit. Watch for qualifiers, negatives and the required
        count.
  \item In a simulation: read it all, plan, configure only what is asked, verify
        with \cmd{show}, save. Partial completion beats none.
  \item v2.0 may ask you to evaluate an AI assistant's output anywhere in the
        paper, not only in domain 5.
  \item Revise against the verbs: 55\% hands-on, so spend the time at a command
        line. Leave the \emph{describe} topics until last, and make subnetting
        automatic.
  \item Cisco publishes 120 minutes and the domain weights. It does not publish
        the question count or the pass mark. Take the official tutorial before
        the day.
\end{tight}
\end{chaptersummary}

\begin{reviewq}
  \item \textbf{(MCQ)} You are unsure of a question and have spent 90 seconds on
        it. What should you do?
        \begin{tight}
          \item[(a)] Flag it and return at the end
          \item[(b)] Eliminate what you can, choose the best remaining option, and
                     move on
          \item[(c)] Leave it blank to avoid a wrong answer
          \item[(d)] Skip ahead and come back if time allows
        \end{tight}
  \item \textbf{(MCQ)} You reach a simulation with 25 minutes remaining and
        several questions still to come. What is the best approach?
        \begin{tight}
          \item[(a)] Skip it to protect time for the remaining questions
          \item[(b)] Complete it properly, verify, and accept the time cost
          \item[(c)] Configure quickly without verifying
          \item[(d)] Answer the questions after it first, then return
        \end{tight}
  \item \textbf{(Short)} Explain why ``I will come back to it'' is the most
        expensive assumption you can carry into this exam.
  \item \textbf{(Short)} Give three question-wording traps and an example of each.
  \item \textbf{(Applied)} Write your own revision plan for the four weeks before
        your exam date, justified against the verb distribution rather than the
        chapter order. State what you will \emph{not} spend time on and why.
  \item \textbf{(Applied)} Set your personal per-question time limit and write
        down what you will do when you hit it on a question you believe you can
        solve. Be specific --- this is the decision the Break \& Fix in this
        chapter turns on, and it must be made in advance.
\end{reviewq}
