\chapter{Command Reference}\label{app:commands}

Grouped by the job you are doing, not alphabetically, because that is how you
will look them up. Commands are shown in the mode they are entered in; the
chapter that teaches each group is named beside it.

\begin{alertbox}[Two conventions used throughout]
Angle brackets mark something you supply: \cmd{<vlan-id>}. A command shown
indented belongs in the sub-mode above it.

\textbf{These are written for IOS and IOS XE and have not been verified on live
hardware.} Run each one on your own platform before relying on it, and check
interface naming --- IOS XE uses \cmd{GigabitEthernet0/0/0}, classic IOS
\cmd{GigabitEthernet0/0}, and a Catalyst switch \cmd{GigabitEthernet1/0/1}.
\end{alertbox}

\cmd{ip route 0.0.0.0 0.0.0.0 <next-hop>} & Default route \\

\cmd{ipv6 router ospf <pid>} \newline \cmd{~~router-id <a.b.c.d>} &
OSPFv3. The router ID is \textbf{mandatory} on an IPv6-only router \\
\rowcolor{lightbg}
\cmd{~~ipv6 ospf <pid> area <n>} & Enables OSPFv3 \emph{on the interface}. There
are no network statements \\
\cmd{~~ip ospf network point-to-point} & No DR election on a two-router link \\
\rowcolor{lightbg}
\cmd{~~standby <grp> ip <vip>} \newline
\cmd{~~standby <grp> priority <n>} \newline
\cmd{~~standby <grp> preempt} &
HSRP. Without \cmd{preempt} a recovered router stays standby \\
\cmd{~~vrrp <grp> ip <vip>} & VRRP. Preempts by default \\
\bottomrule
\end{longtable}
